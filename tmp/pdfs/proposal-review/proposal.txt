   Ethics. No human subjects; walkthroughs use synthetic data
and study accounts; store terms are checked before any automated
retrieval.
  Limitations. Absence is not intent: the study establishes that
a harm is not mentioned, never why, and attributes no motive.

A  Worked Example: Coding One Privacy Policy
This appendix walks through the whole method once, on one small example. The app, CycleMate, is made up: a period and fertility tracker in
which users log the dates of their period, their symptoms, and their mood, and the app predicts their fertile days. Its privacy policy is also
made up and is only five sentences long; real policies are much longer, but are coded in exactly the same way.

 The idea in one sentence. First we write down everything a provider could tell users about harm (the harm tree). Then we record what
 the provider actually tells them (the coding). The difference between the two is the finding.


A.1  Step 1: Draw the harm tree, before reading any policy
Before looking at CycleMate’s text, we write down, for this kind of app, how its data could end up hurting a user. We take these routes from
published research on fertility trackers, not from the provider. Each route has three levels:


    Level             The question it answers                          CycleMate example

   Harm (top)         What could happen to the user?                        She is prosecuted for an abortion.
     Capability (middle)   What could someone do with the data?                      Infer from the logged dates that she was pregnant.
    Component (bottom)  Which data or design choice makes that possible?        The period dates she logs in the app.

Read from the bottom up, this is one path to harm:


               Component             makes possible           Capability                     leads to           Harm
               the period dates she logs                someone infers a pregnancy                      she is prosecuted



Next, every part at the bottom gets a label that says whether the provider could remove it. There are three possibilities:


    Label             Meaning                                       CycleMate example

    Core               The app cannot work without it.                      The period dates; the predicted fertile days.
    Context              Every app on a phone has it.                         The phone’s device identifier.
    Implementation      The provider chose it and could choose otherwise.         Sending data to an analytics company; storing a copy on a
                                                                                               server.

Privacy technologies (encryption, processing on the phone, and so on) can only remove implementation parts. The path above is built only
on a core part: the app exists to store period dates. So no privacy technology can close this path. A provider may still describe its protections
honestly; what it cannot honestly do is promise that they prevent this harm.


A.2  Step 2: Split the policy into units
We split the policy into short pieces, called units, usually one paragraph each. CycleMate’s policy has five:


     P1     “We use the dates you enter to predict your fertile window.”
     P2     “We share device identifiers with analytics partners.”
     P3       “Your data stays on your phone, which protects you from anyone using it against you.”
     P4        “In the unlikely event of a data leak, you might receive unwanted contact.”
     P5       “Your data is safe with us.”


A.3  Step 3: Ask the same three questions of every unit
  A. How far up the tree does the text go? Does it only mention data (component), what someone could do with the data (capability),
    or what could happen to the user (harm)?
  B. If it names a harm, how does it treat it? Plainly (acknowledged), as something improbable (hedged), as nothing to worry about
    (minimised), or as the user’s own problem (deflected)?
  C. If it promises protection, can the protection deliver? Which measure is claimed, which part of the tree does it remove, and does
    the text promise more than that?
The table below gives the answers for CycleMate. The last column explains each decision in plain words.

A.6 Who does what
With 50 apps and four places each, there are too many units to code by hand, so a language model does most of Step 3. For every unit it must
answer questions A and B and say which measure is claimed and how it is phrased, and it must quote the exact words each answer rests on.
For P3, it would return the measure “on the phone”, the phrasing “protects you”, and those two quotes. The model does not decide whether a
promise is an overclaim: that follows from the tree by a fixed rule (harm prevention promised + a path to the harm with only core or context
parts = overclaim). Finally, the author codes a sample of units by hand, without looking at the model’s answers, to measure how often the
model is right and, above all, what it misses.
