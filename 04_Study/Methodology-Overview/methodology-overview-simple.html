Methodology at a glance

A harm can only be called missing from what a provider says if the app could cause it. So the study first fixes which harms are possible, then reads what each app says, and compares the two. Section numbers refer to Chapter 4 of the thesis.
Question

1Research question§1.2

Which privacy harms do health-tracker providers acknowledge or downplay, and how do they frame protection? RQ1 coverage, RQ2 stance, RQ3 protection, RQ4 policy versus interface.

Once,
for all apps

2Literature review§4.2

Search security, privacy and HCI venues and four databases, screen by fixed rules, extract what each study reports.

Gives: the harms and capabilities known for health trackers, and method precedents

3Reference model§4.3, §4.7.1

A generic health tracker and harm trees: which data and functions enable which capabilities, and which harms these lead to. A second person checks every pathway; then all instruments are frozen.

Gives: the yardstick of what is possible

4App sample§4.4

About 50 health trackers on the German market: the most popular eligible apps of Google Play and the App Store, one per provider, plus the health apps preinstalled by the leading phone vendors.

For each
app

5Collect what the app says§4.5

Capture the privacy policy and store listing. Walk through the app by hand: can a user find what is collected, turn sharing off, export and delete?

6Code the text§4.6.1–4.6.2

Two language models label every passage of policy and interface text: what is mentioned, the stance toward each harm, and the protection claimed. A person checks every label that names a harm, a stance or how protection is framed.

7Compare with the model§4.6.3–4.6.5

Which harms apply to this app? How far does its text go for each: silent, data, capability or harm? Would its protection claims actually block the harm?

Across
all apps

8Check the models§4.7.2

Two people code a sample of the text by hand. The models' errors are measured on it, and the reported figures are corrected for them.
