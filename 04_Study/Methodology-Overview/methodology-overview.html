Methodology overview: from the sources to the conclusions

How the thesis answers its research question: which privacy harms health-tracker providers communicate, which they leave out, and how they frame protection. The framework is built once for all apps; the evidence, coding and assessment steps are repeated for each app; the validation covers all coded text. Section numbers refer to Chapter 4 of the thesis, unless a card names another chapter.

Research question and scope

§1.2, §4.1
RQ. What types of downstream privacy harms do health-tracker service providers explicitly acknowledge or downplay when describing their privacy practices or PETs, and how do they frame the protection these technologies offer?
  • RQ1 Coverage: which data flows, capabilities and harms are mentioned; which applicable harms are not
  • RQ2 Stance: acknowledged, qualified, minimised, denied, or shifted to the user
  • RQ3 Protection: claimed measures, their scope and strength, against the reference model
  • RQ4 Surfaces: differences between privacy policy and inspected interface
  • Logic: a harm can be called missing only if the app could cause it, so the study first fixes what is possible, then what is said, then compares the two
  • Scope: health trackers on the German market; what apps communicate, not how they behave; companion apps of wearables, not the devices; no user study
  • Approach: directed content analysis; surfaces compared as in Malki et al.

Literature search

§4.2.1–4.2.2

  • Citation searching: one round backward and forward from four seed papers (OpenCitations, Crossref)
Gives: records with exact strings and hit counts per source (PRISMA-S)

Store search

§4.4.1
  • Domain: health trackers; scope from Hutton et al. (Quantified Self data types) and Malki et al. (reproductive health)
  • Keywords: 15 of Hutton et al. and 12 of Malki et al., each in English and one German translation of the data type
  • Stores: Google Play and Apple App Store, German storefronts, one logged-out session each
Gives: frozen store collections with listing fields (read-only, hashed)

Device vendors

§4.4.3
  • Preinstalled health apps reach users without a download, so ratings understate them
  • Vendor rule: on each platform, the vendor with the largest share of mobile page views in Germany (StatCounter, dated)
  • App rule: health apps the vendor provides and installs on its current phones
Gives: the preinstalled group, fixed by its own rule

Design principles

§4.1, §4.7
  • Instruments are fixed and frozen before collection and coding
  • Language models code everything; people check every high-stakes label
  • A stratified human-coded sample measures the models' errors and corrects the reported totals
  • Every step leaves a record: strings, logs, frozen collections, versioned bundle (§4.8.2)
2 · Selection

Deduplication and screening

§4.2.3
  • Merge by DOI or arXiv ID, or same normalised title with years ≤ 1 apart (≤ 3 for arXiv preprints)
  • Eligibility C1–C4: research article or review; English; in the period; answers an LQ
  • Exclusion rules R1–R8 in fixed order (title, abstract, full text); the first that applies is the reason
  • Every decision logged; missing abstracts taken from OpenAlex by DOI
Gives: included studies and a PRISMA 2020 flow

Eligibility of listings

§4.4.1
  • E1 records the user's own health or body data over time
  • E2 consumer app for the general public
  • E3 free to install   E4 listing in German or English
  • E5 not a DiGA (checked against the BfArM directory)
  • Checked on the German listing, in rank order; each decision logged with its reason

Cross-store matching and ranking

§4.4.2
  • Same app: same branded product for the same use (Steinböck et al.), proposed from several signals, decided by hand
  • Popularity score Pi: Google Play written reviews vi and App Store ratings ri, converted by one factor c
  • German feedback counts are the only count available for the German market; a proxy, tested by sensitivity checks

Provider rule and sensitivity checks

§4.4.3
  • One app per provider: a listing is skipped if its developer is already in the sample
  • Preinstalled group added by its own rule
  • Sensitivity: rerun with one rule changed at a time (developer rule, platform weights, score, search depth, keywords); changed providers and listings reported
3 · Instruments, built once

Extraction and synthesis

§4.2.4
  • Per study: sub-question, app domain, material studied, venue type (fixed keyword rules)
  • From full texts: parts of the RQ informed, harms named, and for LQ1 the capabilities with the data they need and the actor
  • Counts by year and venue, evidence map, prose summary per LQ
Gives: capability table (K1–K8), harms, method precedents

Harm concepts and generic tracker

§4.3.1–4.3.2
  • Vocabulary of Knabenhans et al.: system components → capabilities → harms
  • Harm: impairment of an entity's ability to function, against the user without the modelled pathway
  • Categories: physical, economic, psychological, autonomy, discrimination, reputational, societal; relationship tag
  • Generic health tracker: functions (logging, insights, accounts, analytics, advertising, processors, research, sale, location, sharing, transfers), their data stores and flows, and the actors who reach them

Reference harm trees

§4.3.3
  • Pathway: components that together enable a capability that results in a harm; AND / OR operators
  • Origins: core functionality, digital context, implementation choice; grey for events outside the app
  • Sources: tracker literature plus the review's capabilities; each link documented or argued
  • Added capabilities: a person close to the user, sorting into classes, incapabilities (no delete, export or stop)
  • Machine-readable; a tested script checks every node

Tree check and instrument freeze

§4.7.1
  • Second person checks every pathway: source supports the link; components suffice; the actor has access
  • One versioned bundle: reference model and trees, codebook, prompts and schemas, human coding sheets, sampling manifest, walkthrough protocol, decision forms
  • Later changes logged; affected material recoded
Gives: the frozen yardstick used for every app
4 · Evidence for each app

The sample

§4.4
  • Target: 50 apps, in line with 20–64 in comparable studies
  • Three groups: preinstalled by major vendors, Google Play, Apple App Store
  • Frozen collections and sampling manifest; later work reads these

Privacy policy and store listing

§4.5.1
  • Policy sought through the listing, then the provider's website; saved with SHA-256, capture date and stated date
  • No policy: the app stays and the absence is reported; a shared policy is coded once
  • Language: English version that applies in Germany first, else German; both captured and differences logged
  • Store listing: evidence for applicability only

Cognitive walkthrough

§4.5.2
  • By hand, for every app; throwaway account, synthetic data only; Android if the app is in both stores (proposed)
  • Setup → onboarding before consent → account → tasks T1–T7: what is collected, what is derived, what if exposed, reach the policy, turn off sharing, export, delete
  • Each step asks Q1–Q4 (Wharton et al.); route search stops after five minutes; barriers recorded
Gives: interface text · findability per task · applicability evidence

Coding units

§4.6.1
  • One policy paragraph or one coherent block of interface text, fixed by a script before coding
  • Adjacent text for context; every label quotes its exact span
  • Policy and interface text form one corpus of units
5 · Coding

Three coding axes (codebook)

§4.6.1
ACoverage
  • component, capability, concrete harm, generic risk, additional details
  • polarity kept: asserted, possible or denied
BStance toward a named harm
  • acknowledged, qualified, minimised, responsibility shifted, denied or prevented
  • judged from wording, never from intent
CProtection
  • measure and scope; framing and strength
  • reassurance; potential mismatch (human-reviewed)

Two language models and the human check

§4.6.2
  • Two models from different families, same codebook and prompts, every unit; fixed and frozen before coding (proposed)
  • Stage 1 nodes, harms and risks · Stage 2 stance · Stage 3 protection details
  • A script checks each quoted span; an invalid span counts as a model failure; uncertain never becomes negative
  • Routine labels (components, capabilities, measures): kept if both agree, else kept as uncertain
  • High-stakes labels (harms, risks, stance, framing, reassurance): union of both, and a person checks every one without seeing the models' labels
  • Applicability, minimisation and mismatch: decided by people only
6 · Assessment for each app

Step 1 · Applicability

§4.6.3
  • Each component of the model: present, absent or uncertain, with evidence (policy, listing, walkthrough) and a reason
  • Text shows a feature exists, not that it works; a non-finding does not prove absence
Gives: the app's applicable pathways

Step 2 · Coverage

§4.6.4
  • For each applicable pathway and surface, the highest level the text reaches:
0 silent
1 component
2 capability
3 harm
  • Denied nodes count as mentioned; the denial is shown beside the level
  • Only level 3 carries a stance (Axis B)

Step 3 · Protection claims

§4.6.4
  • The author names the component a claimed measure would remove
  • A script sets it to absent and evaluates the trees again
  • A pathway within the claim's scope that stays open is a potential mismatch candidate; the author decides and writes down why

Validation sample

§4.7.2
  • Target: all coding units of all apps; after the models have coded
  • Strata in order: V1 models disagree · V2 uncertain label · V3 harm vocabulary, no high-stakes label · V4 interface unit · V5 the rest
  • Simple random draw per stratum with a recorded seed
  • Two human coders (author and second coder), blind to model output; Krippendorff's α before discussion
7 · Figures for each research question

Comparison and reporting by research question

§4.6.5
  • RQ1: per harm, only applicable apps with their core sources captured; the app is the primary unit, each app weighted equally
  • RQ2: stance of each named harm, with its evidence
  • RQ3: claimed measures, scope, strength and potential mismatches
  • RQ4: same codes on policy and interface; at onboarding and the privacy settings, text shown directly, behind a link or not found
  • Missing policies, retrieval failures and blocked inspections reported apart; task-specific denominators
  • Results are descriptive

Error estimates and corrections

§4.7.2
  • Precision and recall per code, model and combination rule; beta intervals for small counts
  • Corrected totals: T̂ = ΣU gi + ΣS (yi − gi)/πi
  • App and pathway figures: detection summaries with the unit miss rate beside them
  • Absence claims stated with recall; under 20 reference positives: "not found", not "absent"

Throughout: ethics, availability and limitations

§4.8
Ethics. No human participants: public documents and the researcher's own use of public apps; store terms checked; throwaway accounts and synthetic data only.
Availability. Search strings, screening log, frozen store collections and scripts, captured policies, recordings and transcripts, the instrument bundle and raw model output are kept.
Limitations. Sample favours free, popular apps; dated snapshot and selected paths; trees depend on the literature; unit figures are estimates; English first.
