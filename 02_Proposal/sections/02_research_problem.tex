\section{Research Problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{The gap}

We have a well-developed vocabulary for privacy harms and a well-developed
literature on whether health trackers tell the truth about their data
practices. We do not have a systematic account of \emph{which harms providers
communicate at all}. Prior studies examine the security and privacy of health
trackers, fitness and fertility trackers included, but none maps which harm
types are acknowledged, which are minimised, and which are never mentioned, nor
how PETs are framed --- as secrecy (nobody else sees your data) or as harm
prevention (nothing bad can happen to you).

The gap has a specific shape, and it follows from Section~\ref{sec:background}.
Privacy communication is organised around downstream data practices: who
receives data, for which purposes, on which legal basis, for how long. That is
what the law asks for, and that is what the measurement literature checks. Three
things sit outside this frame:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.4cm,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
  \item[\textbf{Inference}] what can be \emph{derived} from the collected data
    beyond what the user explicitly provided --- pregnancy, illness, mood,
    routine, personality~\parencite{martin2025cookies,niksirat2024wearable}.
  \item[\textbf{Breach}] what would happen to the user if the data were exposed
    to parties who were never intended to see it.
  \item[\textbf{Incident handling}] how the provider detects, responds to, and
    notifies users about security and privacy incidents.
\end{description}

A fourth element cuts across all three: whether any \emph{concrete consequence}
for the user is ever named --- being denied insurance, being outed, being
stalked, being prosecuted --- or whether the text remains at the level of data
flows. Where a provider is silent on all four, the user is left with material
that describes a mechanism and never describes a stake. Users who believe they
are protected may be protected against exactly the harms that the communication
never addressed, and no others. Gabriele and Chiasson's finding that users rate
harmful scenarios as plausible but unlikely, and Cao et al.'s finding that users
feel uninformed and powerless, are both consistent with a communication
environment that supplies no harm vocabulary at
all~\parencite{gabriele2020understanding,cao2024deleted}.

Whether this is in fact what providers do is an empirical question. This thesis
answers it.

\subsection{Research Questions}

The overarching question, as agreed with the supervisor, is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ.} What types of downstream privacy harms do health tracker service
providers explicitly acknowledge or downplay when describing their privacy
practices or PETs, and how do they frame the protection these technologies
offer?
\end{quote}

\noindent It decomposes into four answerable sub-questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.9cm, style=nextline, itemsep=4pt]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{Coverage.} Which types of privacy harm appear in
    the privacy communication of fitness and fertility tracking apps, and which
    are absent? Specifically, how often is inference, breach, incident handling,
    or a concrete consequence for the user named at all?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{Stance.} Where a harm is mentioned, how is it
    treated --- explicitly acknowledged, hedged, minimised, or shifted onto the
    user as their responsibility?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{PET framing.} How is protection framed? Which of
    the three industry principles identified by Martin et al.\ (limiting
    third-party access, preferring inferences over raw data, keeping data on the
    device) do providers invoke, and is the protection presented as secrecy or
    as harm prevention?
  \item[\textbf{RQ4}] \textbf{Consistency across surfaces.} Does the answer to
    RQ1--RQ3 change between the privacy policy, the store listing, the provider
    website and advertisements, and the app interface itself?
\end{description}

RQ1--RQ3 correspond to the three components of the agreed RQ. RQ4 is an
extension that the multi-source corpus makes possible at no additional
collection cost, and it is the sub-question most likely to yield a finding with
direct design implications: a harm named in a policy nobody reads and absent
from the screen where the decision is actually made is, in practice, not
communicated.

\subsection{Scope and Delimitations}

\paragraph{In scope.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Domains:} fitness and activity tracking (including sleep, heart
        rate, and nutrition tracking) and fertility\,/\,period tracking.
  \item \textbf{Market:} the German storefronts of Google Play and the Apple App
        Store. Apps may be internationally operated; the storefront defines the
        sample and the version of the communication that a user in Germany
        actually sees.
  \item \textbf{Material:} privacy policies, store listing descriptions and
        structured privacy disclosures, provider websites and privacy blog
        posts, advertisements encountered during use, and privacy-relevant
        content shown inside the app.
  \item \textbf{Devices:} smartphones and smartphone-paired wearables, analysed
        through the app.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Out of scope, deliberately.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{A user study.} The thesis analyses documents and interfaces. The
        scenario-based survey described in the original thesis offer is a natural
        second phase and is not part of this work (see
        Section~\ref{sec:openpoints}).
  \item \textbf{Verifying whether claims are true.} Traffic analysis and static
        or dynamic app analysis answer a different question, already well served
        by existing
        work~\parencite{huckvale2019assessment,hudig2025intimate,arkalakis2024abandon}.
        This thesis analyses the communication as text and interface.
  \item \textbf{Legal compliance assessment.} Whether a given policy satisfies
        Articles 13--14 GDPR is a legal judgement. The GDPR enters this work as
        context for what providers are obliged to say, not as a yardstick to
        grade them against.
  \item \textbf{Prescription digital health applications} (\emph{Digitale
        Gesundheitsanwendungen}, DiGA) and apps regulated as medical devices.
        Their communication is governed by a separate regime and is not
        comparable to consumer self-tracking apps.
\end{itemize}
