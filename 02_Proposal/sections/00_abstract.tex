Fitness and health tracking apps collect continuous behavioural and
physiological data from which sensitive attributes can be inferred. Providers
communicate privacy across several surfaces --- privacy policies, store listings
and labels, websites, advertisements, and the app interface itself --- but that
communication is organised around downstream data practices: who receives data,
for which purpose, for how long. What it may be silent about is what can be
derived from the data, what would follow for the user if the data were exposed,
and how incidents are handled. We lack a systematic account of which privacy
harms providers name at all. This thesis supplies one. Fifty highly rated
fitness and fertility tracking apps are sampled from the German Google Play and
Apple App Store storefronts; every privacy-relevant text they present is
collected and frozen, and twelve are additionally inspected through a
privacy-focused cognitive walkthrough. Coding units are assigned a harm type
from the Citron and Solove typology, a stance --- acknowledged, hedged,
minimised, deflected, or absent --- and a code for how the protection offered by
privacy-enhancing technologies is framed. Coding is LLM-assisted with versioned
prompts and validated against manual coding of a stratified subsample. The
expected result is a quantified map of which harms are communicated, which are
minimised, and where the communication is simply silent.
