\paragraph{Same app and same provider.} The identity register decides all 946 pairs of a Google
Play and an App Store listing proposed for review: 308 are the same app, 636 different apps, and 2
remain unresolved; counting the 2 as the same app changes no selected listing. In 118 same-app
pairs the two listings name different developers, which therefore count as one provider
(Table~\ref{tab:identity-providers}). Thirteen further developer names were matched to a provider
by hand under condition P3 (Table~\ref{tab:developer-aliases}). No store account fell under two
providers, and no two providers among the candidates reached named the same web domain.

\paragraph{Popularity score.} Of the 189 candidates in both stores, 166 had positive counts in
both. Their counts correlated at Spearman $\rho = 0.80$, and the median ratio of Google Play
reviews to App Store ratings was $m = 0.2364$. With the German platform shares of September 2026,
iOS 27.7\,\% and Android 72.3\,\%~\parencite{statcounter2026os}, the conversion factor is
$c = 0.2364 \times 0.277 / 0.723 \approx 0.0906$: one App Store rating counts as 0.09 Google Play
reviews.

\paragraph{Logging and predicting on the phone (UC1).} The first card names four components:
the health records, the insights computed from them and the history that grows (all core), and
the fact that anyone who can open the phone can read the entries (digital context). Nothing leaves
the phone, yet one capability is already possible: people near the phone read the entries (K9,
Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-uc1}). It leads to three harms, physical (P04), psychological (P08) and
reputational (P19). No component of this subtree is a choice of the provider.

\paragraph{Account and a copy on the provider's servers (UC2).} The second card adds an account, a
copy of the records that may lie in another country, and two events outside the app, an attacker
gaining access and an authority demanding data. Two capabilities become possible: attackers read
stored data (K6), and law enforcement obtains records (K7), either from the server copy or from
the phone itself (CX1, from UC1). They open six pathways to economic, psychological, autonomy, reputational and
societal harm (Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-uc2}). After UC2, six of the seven harm categories have
a tree.

\paragraph{Usage analytics and advertising (UC3).} The third card adds analytics and advertising
services and the identifiers they read. Profiling across services (K5) and the sorting of users
into classes (K10, through an advertising partner and a persistent identifier) become possible,
and K6 gains a route through the analytics service. The four new pathways reach psychological
(P05), autonomy (P13), discrimination (P16) and societal harm (P20) (Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-uc3}).
Discrimination is the last harm to appear; from UC3 on, all seven harm trees exist.

\paragraph{Later build use cases (UC4 to UC7).} The remaining four use cases add pathways to harms
that already have a tree (Figures~\ref{fig:usecase-uc4} to~\ref{fig:usecase-uc7} in
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-use-cases}). Sharing with
other people (UC4) lets insurers and employers use shared or obtained data (K8: P02, P15) and opens
a second route to K9. Recording routes (UC5) lets others locate homes and places (K1: P03).
Processors, research partners and commercial use (UC6) make the inference of personal attributes
(K2: P14) and the re-identification of de-identified records (K4: P17) possible and add three
routes to K6, K8 and K10. The terms of use (UC7) add three incapabilities of the user, to use a
function without agreeing, to delete or take out the data and to stop a flow (I1 to I3: P09 to
P11), all autonomy harms.

\paragraph{Common sub-trees.} Six of the twelve capabilities lead to more than one harm
(Table~\ref{tab:usecase-common}). They are defined once, in the use case that first needs them,
and every harm refers to them, as ceasing service and tracking are shared by the harm trees of
Knabenhans et al.~\parencite[Fig.~5]{knabenhans2026harms}. They carry 15 of the 21 pathways.
The other six capabilities (K1, K2, K4, I1, I2, I3) lead to one harm each.

\paragraph{Check cases (UC8 to UC10).} The same six questions were answered for diet and weight
logging (UC8), mood and smoking-cessation tracking (UC9) and sleep and heart rate synced from a
wearable (UC10). Every answer found a component that the model already holds. None of the three
adds a component, a capability, a route or a pathway (the last rows of
Table~\ref{tab:usecase-steps}), so the construction stopped. Five of the ten answers of the
check cases are documented by a source and five are argued, and the check cases draw on the
same ten papers as the build cases. One item of the sources does not
map: the inference of keystrokes or PINs from the motion sensors of a watch (K3), which needs
the connected device and is outside the study (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\paragraph{The general harm tree.} After the seven build use cases, the tree holds 23
components (3 core, 3 from the digital context, 14 implementation choices and 3 events outside
the app), 12 capabilities and 21 pathways to seven harms. It is the reference model of
Appendix~\ref{app:reference-model}; Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-general} shows how its capabilities
connect to the harms. Twenty of the 23 components enter through an answer that a source
documents for health trackers; three are argued: the growing history (CO3), another person
using the phone (CX1) and an attacker gaining access (EX1). For 14 pathways a source states the
link; 7 are argued from their sources.
Reordering the seven build use cases gives the same components, capabilities and pathways (a
test of the script draws five random orders); only the use case in which an element first appears
changes.
