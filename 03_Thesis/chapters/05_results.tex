\subsection{Search and Screening}

\paragraph{Identification.} The searches ran on 28 October 2026; the final runs
started between 14:46 and 15:10~UTC (Appendix~\ref{app:lit-strings}).
Table~\ref{tab:lit-sources} gives the hits and records of every source. The four
databases returned 2{,}583 hits over the strands, which were 2{,}421 records
once each database's own repeats were merged; IEEE Xplore contributed most of
them (1{,}240), and its S1 query alone returned 1{,}004 hits. The eight venues
were listed completely: 25{,}196 papers, from 372 at SOUPS to 10{,}718 at CHI.
The venue rules selected 85 of them. The German PubMed strand (S5) returned no
record. Merging the database and venue records removed 111 duplicates across
sources and left 2{,}395 records from the searches. The other routes added 585
records that no search had found: 483 from citation searching alone, 64 from the
Methods Digest alone and 38 from the thesis citations alone or from more than
one of these routes.
In total, 2{,}980 records were screened. For 105 of them no abstract was
available, and 371 were screened with an abstract looked up by DOI.

\paragraph{Full texts.} Of the 292 records, 48 were held by the project as
PDFs. For the other 244, open-access copies were sought on 28 October 2026, in
this order: arXiv, Europe PMC, usenix.org, the PoPETs site and the open-access
locations listed by OpenAlex. They yielded 100 files: 52 from arXiv, 30 article
texts from Europe PMC, 15 from other open-access locations, 2 from usenix.org
and 1 from the PoPETs site. One file was a journal issue without the article.
In all, 147 full texts were assessed. Seven failed R8: two are not research
articles in the sense of C1 (a master's thesis and an extended abstract), one
is a two-page paper, and four do not answer a sub-question on reading (a study
of location-based services, interviews on period tracking without a privacy
analysis, a classifier that is not a large language model, and a discussion of
language models for assessing policies without an
evaluation~\parencite{aydin2024assessing}). The other 140 are the review
evidence: 67 answer LQ1, 52 LQ2, 36 LQ3 and 15 LQ4. For 145 records no full text
was obtained: 110 have no open-access location at all and 34 only locations
that returned no PDF to the script, 12 of them in the ACM Digital Library; 69 of
the 145 come from citation searching, 66 from IEEE Xplore and 20 from PubMed.
R7 has not been applied to them, because the university library was not tried.
They remain in the corpus as \emph{awaiting full text}, with the sub-questions
assigned at title and abstract (102 LQ1, 38 LQ2, 13 LQ3, 12 LQ4), and no
statement below rests on them alone.

\paragraph{Existing literature.} All 43 works cited in the thesis before this
review entered the corpus. Sixteen meet the criteria and are review evidence.
The other 27 are in the contextual collection: 10 are not research articles
(R1; web pages, official statistics, a book, a book chapter and software), 5
predate 2014 (R2; among them Solove's taxonomy and Calo's boundaries of privacy
harm), 7 lie outside the domain (R3; among them Citron and Solove's typology,
the harm model of Knabenhans et al.\ and the work on annotation with language
models), and 5 analyse privacy text without language models or risk wording
(R6; OPP-115, Polisis, MAPS, the Data Safety study of Arkalakis et al.\ and the
analysis of Apple's privacy labels by Li et~al.). Five works cited since join
them: the four reporting and review guidelines of
Section~\ref{sec:lit-review} and the paper excluded at full text. Of the 116 Methods Digest
papers, 36 are review evidence, 19 are contextual (cited in the thesis), 60
were excluded and 1 awaits its full text. Citation searching from the four seed
papers yielded 529 DOIs, of which 4 had no metadata; 81 of its 525 records
passed screening, 61 of them found by no other route.

is young: of the 285 records that passed screening and were not excluded at full
text, 143 (50\,\%) were published from 2023 onwards, and although S3 searched
from 2022, the first LQ3 records date from 2023. The records come from a wide
range of venues
(Figure~\ref{fig:lit-venues}): 57 from health or medical journals, 49 from

are preprints, because arXiv was the main open-access source of full texts.

domain with the material each study analysed, coded from titles and abstracts

which part of the thesis RQ each bears on, coded by hand from the full text.

fitness wearables and a design study of risk communication in intimate health
technologies~\parencite{moniz2025toward}; neither analyses what providers
actually write. The closest precedent for RQ3 is a discourse analysis of
Apple's privacy text for its Health app, compared with users'
experiences~\parencite{serttas2026bounded}; it covers one app and codes the
construction of user control, not harms. Eighty studies serve as method

\paragraph{LQ1: harms and capabilities.} The 67 LQ1 studies document
capabilities more often than harms as such: what data an adversary or a
recipient can obtain or infer, and from what. Fifty-one of them were coded with
at least one of eight capability categories, fixed before the full-text coding
(Table~\ref{tab:lit-capabilities}); the table is the input to the harm trees.
Reading stored or transmitted data through insecure Bluetooth, network or
storage practices is the most frequent (20 studies), followed by the profiling
of users by third parties that receive app data (16), often without the policy
saying
so~\parencite{huckvale2015unaddressed,huckvale2019assessment,grundy2019data,tangari2021mobile}.
Location is inferable from shared activity routes and elevation profiles, even
when users hide their homes behind the privacy zones that fitness platforms
offer~\parencite{hassan2018analysis,dhondt2022run}; personality and other
attributes are inferable from tracker data~\parencite{zufferey2023watch};
de-identified activity, gait and heart data can be re-identified; and motion
sensors of smartwatches reveal keystrokes and PINs. For reproductive health,
studies after the overturn of Roe v.~Wade add law enforcement as a recipient:
users were concerned about access by law enforcement and third
parties~\parencite{cao2024deleted}, and the policies of female health apps named
the possible disclosure of personal data to law
enforcement~\parencite{malki2024exploring}. Mental health apps showed
unnecessary permissions, insecure cryptography and leaks of personal data and
credentials~\parencite{iwaya2023privacy}. Only one study names insurers or
employers as users of the data, through participants' concerns. The 102 LQ1
records still awaiting their full text will extend the table.

\paragraph{LQ2: communication.} The 52 LQ2 studies of the review evidence
analyse policies above all: their availability, readability and
quality~\parencite{sunyaev2015availability,robillard2019availability,oloughlin2019reviewing},
their content for menstrual and female health apps~\parencite{shipp2020period,malki2024exploring}
and their match with app behaviour~\parencite{huckvale2019assessment,tangari2021mobile}.
Fewer go beyond the policy: Hutton et al.\ evaluated apps and policies against
heuristics~\parencite{hutton2018assessing}, Malki et al.\ combined policies
with an inspection of the apps~\parencite{malki2024exploring}, Hudig and
Singh studied the data flows of fertility trackers and users'
preferences~\parencite{hudig2025intimate}, and an audit of three period trackers
used them as a user would, against data-protection and intimate-privacy
criteria~\parencite{white2026experiential}. One study analysed store
descriptions together with policies, for depression
apps~\parencite{singh2023assessment}. None codes which harms the communication
names or how it qualifies them. Among the records awaiting their full text, a
discourse analysis of how period- and fertility-tracking companies describe
privacy comes closest to this thesis and must be assessed before the gap can be
stated as confirmed.

\paragraph{LQ3: language models.} The 36 LQ3 studies use large language models
to annotate, classify, extract from or assess privacy policies and terms: data
practices, contextual integrity, compliance with privacy laws or GDPR
transparency, contradictions within policies or with privacy labels, and
answers to users' questions~\parencite{rodriguez2024llm,chanenson2025automating,xie2025evaluating}.
Each reports an empirical evaluation, as LQ3 required. A few derive risk ratings
for users from policy clauses, but none codes how the provider words or
qualifies harms, which is the coding task of this thesis. Their validation
designs are the precedents for the LLM-assisted coding; the cautions of Baumann
et al.\ and Törnberg on configuration choices and validation remain in the
contextual collection~\parencite{baumann2025llm,tornberg2024best}.

\paragraph{LQ4: framing.} The 15 LQ4 studies analyse how texts present risk or
protection: vagueness and clarity of policies~\parencite{lebanoff2018automatic},
hedging and the downplaying of risk in breach
notifications~\parencite{zou2019might}, the effect of framing in privacy notices,
the communication of differential privacy, the transparency of genetic-testing
policies~\parencite{doan2024transparency}, the promises made for
privacy-enhancing techniques~\parencite{martin2025cookies}, risk communication
in intimate health technologies~\parencite{moniz2025toward}, privacy
washing~\parencite{brackin2026privacy}, disclosures hidden in regional sections
of policies, and the construction of control in Apple's Health privacy
text~\parencite{serttas2026bounded}. Only the last analyses the actual text of a
health tracking provider; the health-related framing studies are otherwise
experiments or design studies.

\paragraph{Answer to the review question and gaps.} The confirmed evidence
documents capabilities and harms for health tracking apps well, especially
insecure handling of data, third-party profiling, location and attribute
inference and re-identification, and it shows how policies of such apps have
been sampled and coded. It barely shows how providers word the harms they
acknowledge, whether they downplay them, or how they frame the protection of
PETs: one study analyses how a single provider constructs control in its privacy
text, and no study of the review evidence combines harm coverage, stance and
protection framing across several surfaces of health tracking apps. The
language-model studies validate the annotation of data practices and
compliance, not the coding of harms or stance. Three limits qualify this
answer. First, 145 records that passed title and abstract still await their
full text, most of them from IEEE Xplore and from citation searching, which
mainly found ACM papers; the review evidence therefore leans towards open-access
work (65 of the 140 studies are on arXiv). Second, the domain and surface codes
of the evidence map come from keyword rules on titles and abstracts and are
coarse. Third, the ACM Digital Library, Scopus, Web of Science and Google
Scholar were not searched, and five venues were filtered on titles only.

\paragraph{Collection.} The App Store was searched on 23 October 2026 between
19:46 and 19:53~UTC\@. Of the 49 queries, 48 returned between 64 and 190 results;
\emph{reproduktive Gesundheit} returned none. The first 30 positions held 1{,}440
hits and 848 distinct listings. Google Play was searched on 24 October 2026
between 15:07 and 15:29~UTC\@. Each query returned between 8 and 50 results, 1{,}178
in total; the first 30 positions held 1{,}138 hits and 645 distinct apps, and
every hit carried an app ID\@. The product pages of all 645 apps were read on
24 and 25 October 2026; one page that timed out was read again, so none is
missing.

\paragraph{Preinstalled apps.} The vendor shares for September 2026, read on
22 October 2026, give Samsung 39.3\,\%, Apple 27.6\,\% and Xiaomi 21.4\,\% of
German mobile page views, and Huawei and Google about 1.5\,\% each
\parencite{statcounter2026vendor}. Apple is the only vendor of iOS (27.7\,\% of
page views). On Android (72.3\,\%), Samsung leads with 54\,\% of the
platform's page views, followed by Xiaomi with 30\,\%
\parencite{statcounter2026os}. The leading vendors are therefore Apple and
Samsung. Their qualifying apps are Apple Health and Apple Fitness on the iPhone
and Samsung Health on Samsung phones. The rule therefore yields three
preinstalled apps, which leaves 47 places for the ranking. A threshold of 10\,\%
of all page views would admit Xiaomi as well, but no preinstalled tracker could
be identified on Xiaomi's international software, so the three apps are the same
under that rule and under a rule that covers 80\,\% of Android. All three have store listings, which
were removed from the ranking; Apple Health, for instance, has 2{,}134 ratings
in the German App Store.

\paragraph{Same app in both stores.} In the frames of the selection and its
checks, 946 pairs of a Google Play and an App Store listing were proposed for
review: 543 because they share only the developer, the others because they
share the app identifier, the title or description text. 308 pairs were
decided to be the same app, 636 two different apps, and 2 remain unresolved;
counting the 2 as the same app changes no selected listing. Official pages
that link both listings support 8 of the same-app pairs, and English store
names a further 18. Among the candidates reached, the decisions correct three
pairings of the earlier title rule: Nike Run Club and the BabyCenter pregnancy
app had been paired with another app of their publisher (Nike Training Club;
What to Expect), and Leap's weight-loss apps for men and for women with each
other. In 118 of the same-app pairs the two listings name different
developers, which therefore count as one provider
(Table~\ref{tab:identity-providers}). Three of these pairs join three
providers: the websites of Simple Design and ABISHKKING present the same
period, weight-loss and step-counter apps, one with their Google Play and one
with their App Store listings, and the step counter's App Store listing
belongs to the Leap Health account.

\paragraph{German-market popularity score.} Of the candidates, 189 apps were
found in both stores. The calibration used the 166 with positive rating and
review counts in both stores. Their counts correlated at Spearman 0.80, and
the median ratio was $m = 0.236412$. With the German platform shares of
September 2026, Android 72.3\,\% and iOS
27.7\,\%~\parencite{statcounter2026os}, the conversion factor was
\[
  c = 0.236412\times\frac{0.277}{0.723}\approx 0.090575.
\]
Thus, one App Store rating contributed approximately 0.09 score units and one
Play review contributed one unit. For example, komoot had 417{,}443 German App
Store ratings and 99{,}884 German Play reviews. Using the unrounded factor,
its score was $P_{\mathrm{komoot}}\approx137{,}694$. This is a combined feedback
score, not a user count; the script ranked candidates with unrounded scores.
Across all 644 Google Play candidates, the German review count and the
worldwide rating count correlated at 0.95, yet they rank the leading apps
differently: among the 100 apps with the most installs worldwide, the German
review count ranged from 5 to 99{,}884.

\paragraph{Screening and selection.} Table~\ref{tab:sample-flow} gives the
cascade. Merging the two stores' listings gave 1{,}301 candidates. In descending
order of German-market popularity score, 73 candidates were reached before
the 47 places were filled.
Of these, 12 were excluded at screening because the app records no health or
body data over time (E1), and 14 were skipped because their developer was
already in the sample. Developer names that differ between the two stores were
matched by hand for 13 providers, among them Clue, Pregnancy+, Fddb and
Headspace, each on the evidence listed in Table~\ref{tab:developer-aliases};
no store account was found under two providers, and no two providers among the
73 candidates reached named the same web domain. Across the main selection and all sensitivity checks, 276
listings were screened: 157 were eligible and 119 excluded (E1~114, E2~1,
E3~2, E4~1, E5~1).

\section{Reference Model}
\label{sec:results-model}
The procedure of Sections~\ref{sec:generic-model} and~\ref{sec:harm-trees} yields a model with
22 components: 3 core, 3 from the digital context, 13 implementation choices and 3 events
outside the app. They enable 12 capabilities, which lead to the 7 harm categories along 21
pathways. For 14 pathways a source states the link; 7 are argued from their sources. One
capability of the review's table stays out (K3, keystrokes from smartwatch sensors), because it
needs the connected device. Appendix~\ref{app:reference-model} lists the complete model. A tested
script checks that every node a tree uses exists and that every component is used.

\subsection{Test Case and Material}
The test app is Clue, a cycle tracker by BioWink GmbH in Berlin, at rank 12 of the sample
(Appendix~\ref{app:app-sample}). Its English privacy policy, which states that the English version
prevails, was captured on 1 November 2026 at 22:11~UTC. Its own date is 29 September 2026. The script
split it into 178 units of 9{,}427 words. The policy links to a supplement for US residents, which was
not coded under the rule of Section~\ref{sec:material}. \highlight[orange!25]{No German version was
captured for this test.}\todo{The language rule asks for both versions where they exist. None was

\subsection{Which Harms Apply}
Of the 22 components, the policy shows 17 as present and 1 as absent; 4 stay uncertain
(Table~\ref{tab:test-applicability}). Evaluating the trees gives 13 pathways that apply, 7 that stay
uncertain and 1 that does not apply. All 7 harm categories apply. The one closed pathway is P03,
stalking or burglary from known places: the policy states that precise location is not collected, so
K1 lacks its necessary condition. The tree can therefore close a pathway, and it does so from the
provider's own text, which the study does not verify. The uncertain pathways run through K7 and K8,
which depend on outside events, and through I1 and I2, which only the app can show.

stand-in coding agree on this. Of the 13 applicable pathways, 12 stop at the capability and 1 at the

\subsection{Stance (RQ2)}
The policy names no harm, so it carries no stance under the codebook: stance attaches only to a named
harm. Dismissive wording appears without one. On a legal basis for processing, the policy says:
\enquote{We do not believe there is anything you could have against
this}~\parencite[\S3.5]{clue2026privacy}. Under the rule of Section~\ref{sec:axes} this is reassurance,
not minimisation. The one named harm of the test lies in the invented interface \synthtag: unit W05
says that a shared calendar could reveal a pregnancy, and leaves the choice to the user. All three
runs code it as acknowledged and as responsibility shifted. Two also return qualified, which the
review rejects, as \enquote{could} says nothing about likelihood. The stance stage thus ran, but on
one invented sentence, so this test says nothing about how well it works.

\subsection{Protection Claims (RQ3)}
The review examined eight groups of claims (Table~\ref{tab:test-claims}). It reads all of them as framing
protection as data flow, and none as preventing a specific bad outcome, which fits a policy that names no
harm. Two are proposed as potential mismatches. C2 says researchers can never identify the user, as
names and e-mail addresses are erased and a random ID assigned. Figure~\ref{fig:test-cut} shows why this
leaves the pathway open: K4 needs the shared record and its length, and the measure removes neither.
Studies re-identify individuals from de-identified activity and heart data
(Table~\ref{tab:lit-capabilities}); none tests cycle data, so the claim may still hold in practice. C3
says there is no profiling, while the same policy describes recommendations from tracked data and an
advertising partner reporting age groups. The policy does not define the term. It may have a narrower
legal meaning, on which the study makes no judgement.

C1 is the instructive non-mismatch. The pledge that tracked health data never reaches advertisers is
narrow, and device IDs and app events still do. A partner that learns that a device has a cycle app
installed can sort its user into a class (K10). This is a real pathway, but it lies outside what the
pledge promises, so the rule of Section~\ref{sec:assessment} does not count it. It appears under RQ1 instead:
the pathway applies, and the policy states the flow but no consequence.

\paragraph{Where it was stable.} On the central question all runs agree: no named harm in the policy, one in the
invented unit. The paraphrased prompt returned a similar number of labels (163, against 156 to 175) and the same
harm, with an overlap of 0.74 with the labels of at least two runs.

\paragraph{Where it was not.} About a quarter of the stage~1 labels in the union were returned by one run only (47
of 203), and 113 of the 487 labels were marked as unsure. Status differed between runs for the same label: for
U140, one run read K6 as denied and two as possible. At stage~3, the share of claims framed as preventing harm ranged
from 6 to 20 per run, and absolute strength from 24 to 36. Practice commitments dominated (41 to 46 per run), as
many routine retention sentences were coded as commitments.

\paragraph{What it missed and over-read.} The model mapped some capabilities to the wrong node. It read a chat profile
and a contract term on IP addresses as K4, which the review rejected because they do not concern the data of that
pathway. It returned no K10 for the recommendation example in U093. Six of its capability and component labels were rejected
in review, so its output cannot be used without that review. The validation asks for 20 positives per code. This policy
has no positive for harms, so recall on harms cannot be estimated from it. The review trigger therefore fires: a person has
to read the whole policy. In this test the stand-in did so, and the author still has to. All runs together used about one
million tokens, an upper bound that includes the overhead of the agents.

\subsection{Assessment of the Case}
\label{sec:results-assessment}
For this one policy, the procedure gives a clear answer to each question that the real material can answer:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} All seven harm categories apply, along 13 pathways. The policy is detailed about data flows and
    often names what someone could do. It names no consequence for the user.
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} No stance can be coded, as no harm is named. One dismissive sentence is coded as reassurance.
  \item \textbf{RQ3.} Claims are framed as data flow. Two of eight groups are proposed as potential mismatches, and three
    as not assessable.
  \item \textbf{RQ4.} Not answered: the only interface in the test is invented \synthtag.
\end{itemize}
The case also challenges the expected gap. This policy is more open about limits than the gap suggests: it states that
surveillance powers abroad are not bound by the contract clauses, that some flows have no opt-out, and that security
cannot be guaranteed. Its silence begins one step later, at what these limits could mean for the person.
