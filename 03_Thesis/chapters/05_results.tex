\paragraph{Collection.} The App Store was searched on 23 October 2026 between
19:46 and 19:53~UTC\@. Of the 49 queries, 48 returned between 64 and 190 results;
\emph{reproduktive Gesundheit} returned none. The first 30 positions held 1{,}440
hits and 848 distinct listings. Google Play was searched on 24 October 2026
between 15:07 and 15:29~UTC\@. Each query returned between 8 and 50 results, 1{,}178
in total; the first 30 positions held 1{,}138 hits and 645 distinct apps, and
every hit carried an app ID\@. The product pages of all 645 apps were read on
24 and 25 October 2026; one page that timed out was read again, so none is
missing.

\paragraph{Same app in both stores.}\todo{These counts follow the earlier proposals by shared
developer, identifier, title or text. Redo them with candidate pairs by provider (privacy-policy
controller) and without the provider rule (D2, 16 Nov).} In the frames of the selection and its
checks, 946 pairs of a Google Play and an App Store listing were proposed for
review: 543 because they share only the developer, the others because they
share the app identifier, the title or description text. 308 pairs were
decided to be the same app, 636 two different apps, and 2 remain unresolved;
counting the 2 as the same app changes no selected listing. Official pages
that link both listings support 8 of the same-app pairs, and English store
names a further 18. Among the candidates reached, the decisions correct three
pairings of the earlier title rule: Nike Run Club and the BabyCenter pregnancy
app had been paired with another app of their publisher (Nike Training Club;
What to Expect), and Leap's weight-loss apps for men and for women with each
other. In 118 of the same-app pairs the two listings name different
developers, which therefore count as one provider
(Table~\ref{tab:identity-providers}). Three of these pairs join three
providers: the websites of Simple Design and ABISHKKING present the same
period, weight-loss and step-counter apps, one with their Google Play and one
with their App Store listings, and the step counter's App Store listing
belongs to the Leap Health account.

\paragraph{German-market popularity score.} Of the candidates, 189 apps were
found in both stores. The calibration used the 166 with positive rating and
review counts in both stores. Their counts correlated at Spearman 0.80, and
the median ratio was $m = 0.236412$. With the German platform shares of
September 2026, Android 72.3\,\% and iOS
27.7\,\%~\parencite{statcounter2026os}, the conversion factor was
\[
  c = 0.236412\times\frac{0.277}{0.723}\approx 0.090575.
\]
Thus, one App Store rating contributed approximately 0.09 score units and one
Play review contributed one unit. For example, komoot had 417{,}443 German App
Store ratings and 99{,}884 German Play reviews. Using the unrounded factor,
its score was $P_{\mathrm{komoot}}\approx137{,}694$. This is a combined feedback
score, not a user count; the script ranked candidates with unrounded scores.
Across all 644 Google Play candidates, the German review count and the
worldwide rating count correlated at 0.95, yet they rank the leading apps
differently: among the 100 apps with the most installs worldwide, the German
review count ranged from 5 to 99{,}884.

\paragraph{Screening and selection.} Table~\ref{tab:sample-flow} gives the
cascade. Merging the two stores' listings gave 1{,}301 candidates. In descending
order of German-market popularity score, 73 candidates were reached before
the 47 ranked places (50 minus the three preinstalled apps) were filled.
Of these, 12 were excluded at screening because the app records no health or
body data over time (E1), and 14 were skipped because their developer was
already in the sample. Developer names that differ between the two stores were
matched by hand for 13 providers, among them Clue, Pregnancy+, Fddb and
Headspace, each on the evidence listed in Table~\ref{tab:developer-aliases};
no store account was found under two providers, and no two providers among the
73 candidates reached named the same web domain. Across the main selection and all sensitivity checks, 276
listings were screened: 157 were eligible and 119 excluded (E1~114, E2~1,
E3~2, E4~1, E5~1).

\paragraph{Preinstalled apps.} The vendor shares for September 2026, read on
22 October 2026, give Samsung 39.3\,\%, Apple 27.6\,\% and Xiaomi 21.4\,\% of
German mobile page views, and Huawei and Google about 1.5\,\% each
\parencite{statcounter2026vendor}. Apple is the only iOS vendor (27.7\,\% of page
views). On Android (72.3\,\%), Samsung leads with 54\,\% of the platform's page
views, ahead of Xiaomi with 30\,\% \parencite{statcounter2026os}. The leading
vendors are therefore Apple and Samsung, and three of their apps qualify: Apple
Health and Apple Fitness on the iPhone and Samsung Health on Samsung phones. A
threshold of 10\,\% of all page views, or of 80\,\% of Android, would add Xiaomi,
but no preinstalled tracker was found on Xiaomi's international software, so the
three apps stay the same. Their store listings were removed from the ranking
(Table~\ref{tab:sample-flow}); Apple Health, for instance, has 2{,}134 ratings in
the German App Store.

\paragraph{Sample composition.} The ranking contributed 47 apps from 47
providers: 36 listed in both stores, 5 only on Google Play and 6 only on the
App Store. The three preinstalled apps bring the sample to 50. The ranked apps
had 471 to 447{,}096 German App Store ratings (42 listings) and 1{,}486 to
99{,}884 German Google Play reviews (41 listings). All were free to install, and
all 41 Google Play listings link to a privacy policy. Of the 47, 42 appeared
within the first ten positions of a query and 5 between positions 11 and 20.

\paragraph{Logging and predicting on the phone (UC1).} The first card names four components:
the health records, the insights computed from them and the history that grows (all core), and
the fact that anyone who can open the phone can read the entries (digital context). Nothing leaves
the phone, yet one capability is already possible: people near the phone read the entries (K9,
Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-uc1}). It leads to three harms, physical (P04), psychological (P08) and
reputational (P19). No component of this subtree is a choice of the provider.

\paragraph{Account and a copy on the provider's servers (UC2).} The second card adds an account, a
copy of the records that may lie in another country, and two events outside the app, an attacker
gaining access and an authority demanding data. Two capabilities become possible: attackers read
stored data (K6), and law enforcement obtains records (K7), either from the server copy or from
the phone itself (CX1, from UC1). They open six pathways to economic, psychological, autonomy, reputational and
societal harm (Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-uc2}). After UC2, six of the seven harm categories have
a tree.

\paragraph{Usage analytics and advertising (UC3).} The third card adds analytics and advertising
services and the identifiers they read. Profiling across services (K5) and the sorting of users
into classes (K10, through an advertising partner and a persistent identifier) become possible,
and K6 gains a route through the analytics service. The four new pathways reach psychological
(P05), autonomy (P13), discrimination (P16) and societal harm (P20) (Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-uc3}).
Discrimination is the last harm to appear; from UC3 on, all seven harm trees exist.

\paragraph{Later build use cases (UC4 to UC7).} The remaining four use cases add pathways to harms
that already have a tree (Figures~\ref{fig:usecase-uc4} to~\ref{fig:usecase-uc7} in
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-use-cases}). Sharing with
other people (UC4) lets insurers and employers use shared or obtained data (K8: P02, P15) and opens
a second route to K9. Recording routes (UC5) lets others locate homes and places (K1: P03).
Processors, research partners and commercial use (UC6) make the inference of personal attributes
(K2: P14) and the re-identification of de-identified records (K4: P17) possible and add three
routes to K6, K8 and K10. The terms of use (UC7) add three incapabilities of the user, to use a
function without agreeing, to delete or take out the data and to stop a flow (I1 to I3: P09 to
P11), all autonomy harms.

\paragraph{Common sub-trees.} Six of the twelve capabilities lead to more than one harm
(Table~\ref{tab:usecase-common}). They are defined once, in the use case that first needs them,
and every harm refers to them, as ceasing service and tracking are shared by the harm trees of

The other six capabilities (K1, K2, K4, I1, I2, I3) lead to one harm each.

\paragraph{Check cases (UC8 to UC10).} The same six questions were answered for diet and weight
logging (UC8), mood and smoking-cessation tracking (UC9) and sleep and heart rate synced from a
wearable (UC10). Every answer found a component that the model already holds. None of the three
adds a component, a capability, a route or a pathway (the last rows of
Table~\ref{tab:usecase-steps}), so the construction stopped. Five of the ten answers of the
check cases are documented by a source and five are argued, and the check cases draw on the
same ten papers as the build cases. One item of the sources does not
map: the inference of keystrokes or PINs from the motion sensors of a watch (K3), which needs
the connected device and is outside the study (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

\paragraph{The general harm tree.} After the seven build use cases, the tree holds 23
components (3 core, 3 from the digital context, 14 implementation choices and 3 events outside
the app), 12 capabilities and 21 pathways to seven harms. It is the reference model of
Appendix~\ref{app:reference-model}; Figure~\ref{fig:usecase-general} shows how its capabilities
connect to the harms. Twenty of the 23 components enter through an answer that a source
documents for health trackers; three are argued: the growing history (CO3), another person
using the phone (CX1) and an attacker gaining access (EX1). For 14 pathways a source states the
link; 7 are argued from their sources.
Reordering the seven build use cases gives the same components, capabilities and pathways (a
test of the script draws five random orders); only the use case in which an element first appears
changes.
