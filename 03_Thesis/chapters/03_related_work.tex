\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:related-work}

Four strands of research bear on this study: the privacy harms of health
tracking, the privacy communication of health apps, the wording of risk and
protection in privacy texts, and the analysis of such texts with language
models. Each paragraph closes with the question its strand leaves open and the
research question that takes it up.

\paragraph{Harms of health tracking.} What others can learn from tracker data
is well documented. Salehzadeh Niksirat et al.\ survey the research on wearable
activity trackers and collect the inferences and threats that their data
allow~\parencite{niksirat2024wearable}. Shared workout routes reveal protected
locations such as a user's home, even behind the privacy zones that fitness
platforms offer~\parencite{hassan2018analysis,dhondt2022run}. Health apps also
pass data on: 33 of 36 depression and smoking-cessation apps sent data to third
parties~\parencite{huckvale2019assessment}, and the traffic of eight fertility
trackers reached organisations in several
countries~\parencite{hudig2025intimate}. For reproductive health, the
literature also names who might act on the data. After the overturn of Roe
v.~Wade, period-tracker users in the United States were concerned about access
by law enforcement and third parties~\parencite{cao2024deleted}. Fitness
tracker users, by contrast, judged many harm scenarios plausible but
unlikely~\parencite{gabriele2020understanding}.

This evidence comes from attacks, measurements and users' accounts. It has not been assembled into a
reference of the harms that apply to a health tracker, and it has not been
compared with what providers tell their users. RQ1 builds this reference
(Section~\ref{sec:framework}) and measures which of the applicable harms each
app mentions and which it leaves out.

\paragraph{Privacy communication of health apps.} Most studies of what health
apps communicate examine the privacy policy. Some assess the policy itself.
Among 30 Android menstrual apps, the policies mostly did not cover
reproductive data, although the apps needed these data to
work~\parencite{shipp2020period}. Others compare the policy with the app's
behaviour. Of 28 apps that sent data to Google, only 12 disclosed
this~\parencite{huckvale2019assessment}. Few studies include the interface.
Malki et al.\ combined the policies and Data Safety sections of 20 female
health apps with a usability inspection and found inconsistencies between
policies and app features, flawed consent and deletion mechanisms and covert
collection of sensitive data~\parencite{malki2024exploring}. Statements about
consequences appear only in passing. In the same study, 13 of 19 policies said
that law enforcement or other authorities might access user data but explained
little about when~\parencite[\S4.2.5]{malki2024exploring}. In an assessment of
34 health apps and e-tools, 8 policies warned of privacy risks from services
outside the provider's control~\parencite{lamonica2021privacy}.

These studies code data practices: what is collected, who receives it and whether the
policy matches the app. Harms enter as the researchers' threat model, not as
something the provider's text is examined for. RQ1 therefore codes harms,
together with the capabilities and data flows that lead to them, as units of
the provider's text. RQ4 compares what the policy and the interface of the same
app say.

\paragraph{Wording of risk and protection.} A text can disclose a practice and
still blur what follows from it. Zou et al.\ analysed 161 data breach
notifications: 112 (70\,\%) used hedge terms such as \emph{maybe} or
\emph{likely}, and only 9 linked the exposed data to concrete misuse
scenarios~\parencite[\S5]{zou2019might}. Protection claims raise a similar
concern, because privacy-enhancing technologies can work as described and still
permit the harms that are inherent in a
functionality~\parencite{martin2025cookies,knabenhans2026harms}. Ebert et al.\
argue that disclosing data practices alone leaves users uncertain about
potential harms and call for \enquote{risk
transparency}~\parencite{ebert2026procedures}. Moniz and Almeida likewise
recommend that intimate health technologies inform users of the specific risks
of the processing~\parencite{moniz2025toward}.

For health trackers, the evidence on wording is incidental, and none of the studies reviewed here relates a
claimed protection to the pathways to harm it would have to interrupt. RQ2
codes how a named harm is worded, with labels adapted from Zou et al.\
(Section~\ref{sec:axes}), and RQ3 checks each protection claim against the harm
trees.

\paragraph{Language models.} Language models can annotate privacy policies at
scale. Rodriguez et al.\ evaluated ChatGPT and Llama~2 on established corpora of
annotated policies and report F1 scores above 93\,\% for identifying data
practices~\parencite{rodriguez2024llm}. Cory et al.\ annotate policies word by
word against 21 transparency requirements derived from the
GDPR~\parencite{cory2026wordlevel}. These tasks concern what a text discloses,
not which harms it names or how it words them. They inform the design and
validation of the coding in this study (Section~\ref{sec:llm}) and do not
motivate a research question of their own.

\paragraph{Research gap.} Taken together, the four strands leave one question
open: what do providers of health trackers tell users about the harms their
data can lead to? Harms are documented but not compared with providers'
statements. Communication is coded for practices, not harms. The wording of
risk and protection has been studied outside health tracking. RQ1 to RQ4
address this question.
