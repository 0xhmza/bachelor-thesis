\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:related-work}

Four strands of research bear on the research questions.

\paragraph{Harms.} A survey of 236 studies of wearable activity trackers
collects the inferences and threats their data allow~\parencite{niksirat2024wearable}.
Individual studies show that shared routes reveal home locations, that
de-identification protects poorly against re-identification and that apps pass
data to third parties. For
reproductive health, users and analysts name law-enforcement access, stigma,
targeted advertising and intimate harms. These findings come from attacks, measurements and users' accounts. They have not been
assembled into a reference of the harms that apply to a health tracker, nor
compared with what providers tell users. RQ1 builds this reference and measures
which applicable harms each app mentions.

\paragraph{Communication.} The most studied surface is the privacy policy, for
its availability, readability and completeness, and against app traffic and
store labels, where large gaps appear. Few
studies look at the interface. Malki et al.\ combined policies, Data Safety
sections and a usability inspection of 20 female health apps and found
inconsistencies, flawed consent mechanisms and covert collection; 13 of 19
policies said that law enforcement might access data but explained little about
when~\parencite{malki2024exploring}. Apart from such isolated findings, studies
code practices, not harms. RQ1 therefore codes harms, capabilities and data
flows as units of the provider's text, and RQ4 compares policy and interface.

\paragraph{Wording.} Privacy texts can disclose a practice and still blur its
consequences, through vague terms, strategic indeterminacy and hedges. In breach
notices, hedge terms appeared in 70\,\% of 161 notices and only 9 linked the
exposed data to concrete misuse~\parencite{zou2019might}. Studies of
protection claims show that privacy-enhancing technologies still permit the
harms inherent in a functionality, and recent authors call for
\enquote{risk transparency}. For health trackers the evidence is
incidental, and no study relates a claimed protection to the pathways it would
have to interrupt. RQ2 codes how a named harm is worded, with labels adapted from Zou
et al.\ (Section~\ref{sec:axes}), and RQ3 checks each protection claim against
the harm trees.

\paragraph{Language models.} Language models are evaluated against human
annotation for the data practices in privacy
policies~\parencite{rodriguez2024llm}, and further studies apply them to
GDPR transparency requirements and store-label comparison. These tasks concern
what is disclosed, not which harms a text names or how it words them; they
inform the design and validation of the coding (Section~\ref{sec:llm}).

\paragraph{Research gap.} What health tracking providers tell users about the
harms their data can lead to remains open: harms are documented but not compared
with providers' statements, and communication is coded for practices, not harms.
RQ1 to RQ4 take it up.
