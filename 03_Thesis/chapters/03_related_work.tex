\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:related-work}

Four strands of research bear on the research questions. The first documents
which privacy harms health tracking can cause (Section~\ref{sec:rw-harms}). The
second analyses what health apps communicate about privacy
(Section~\ref{sec:rw-communication}). The third studies how privacy texts word
risk and protection (Section~\ref{sec:rw-framing}). The fourth uses language
models to analyse privacy text (Section~\ref{sec:rw-llm}). Each section ends with
the question the strand leaves open and the research question that takes it up;
Section~\ref{sec:rw-gap} brings the four together.

\section{Privacy Harms of Health Tracking}
\label{sec:rw-harms}

\paragraph{Documented capabilities.} Security and privacy research has shown in
detail what others can learn from the data of health trackers. A survey of
wearable activity trackers collects the inferences and threats this data
allows~\parencite{niksirat2024wearable}. Shared routes reveal home locations
even behind the privacy zones that fitness platforms
offer~\parencite{hassan2018analysis,dhondt2022run}, and elevation profiles
alone can reveal where an activity took place~\parencite{meteriz2024learning}.
The privacy controls of these platforms are seldom used: an analysis of 197{,}873
public activity records on Strava and Garmin Connect found a gap between the
controls available and their adoption~\parencite{ioannou2026options}.
Personality traits can be inferred from tracker data~\parencite{zufferey2023watch},
and smartwatch motion sensors reveal what a user types on a
keyboard~\parencite{liu2015good}. De-identification offers little protection: a
systematic review of 72 studies found correct re-identification rates from
wearable data of typically 86 to 100\,\%~\parencite{chikwetu2023deidentification}.
Beyond inference, health apps pass data to third parties. Of 20{,}991 health
apps on Google Play, 88\,\% contained code that could collect user
data~\parencite{tangari2021mobile}, and depression and smoking-cessation apps
sent data to Google and Facebook services that their policies did not
name~\parencite{huckvale2019assessment}.

\paragraph{Harms named by users and analysts.} For reproductive health, the
literature names the actors and consequences more directly. Mehrnezhad and
Almeida trace the risks of fertility apps to the mismanagement, misuse and
misappropriation of intimate data~\parencite{mehrnezhad2021caring}, and Erickson
et al.\ describe the threats that arise from the misuse of femtech data before
analysing its flows~\parencite{erickson2022expecting}. After the overturn of Roe
v.~Wade, period-tracker users feared access by law enforcement and third
parties~\parencite{cao2024deleted}. Interviewed users of female health apps
named criminalisation, distress from social stigma, third-party sharing and
targeted advertising~\parencite{kaleva2026political}. Fitness tracker users
judged harmful data uses plausible but often
unlikely~\parencite{gabriele2020understanding}. Hammond and Burdon argue that
menstrual apps cause intimate harms that disrupt users' relationships, beyond the
loss of control that information privacy law
addresses~\parencite{hammond2024intimate}.

\paragraph{Harms as a unit of analysis.} Legal scholarship separates a privacy
violation from the harm that follows from it and sorts harms into
types~\parencite{solove2006taxonomy,calo2011boundaries,citron2021harms}. Harm
trees make this distinction usable for a system analysis: they connect system
components, through the capabilities they give an actor, to the harms that can
result~\parencite{de2016privacy,de2017refinement}. Knabenhans et al.\ apply harm
trees to the European Digital Identity Framework and show which harms its
privacy-enhancing technologies leave possible~\parencite{knabenhans2026harms}.
Taxonomies of harm are also used to code reported incidents: one study coded 369
privacy incidents reported by members of the public and found that most of the
harms were fear and a loss of psychological safety rather than tangible
damage~\parencite{chapman2025beyond}.

\paragraph{Open question.} This literature establishes what can go wrong. Its
findings come from attacks, measurements, the analyses of researchers and the
accounts of users. They have not been assembled into a reference of the harms
that apply to a health tracking app, and they have not been used to ask which of
these harms providers themselves tell their users about. Harm trees have been
applied to a digital identity system, not to consumer health
apps~\parencite{knabenhans2026harms}. RQ1 builds such a reference for health
trackers (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) and measures, for each app, which of the
applicable harms its policy and interface mention and which they leave out.

\section{Privacy Communication of Health Apps}
\label{sec:rw-communication}

\paragraph{Privacy policies.} The most studied surface is the privacy policy.
Early studies asked whether health apps had a policy at all and how readable and
complete it was~\parencite{sunyaev2015availability,robillard2019availability,oloughlin2019reviewing}.
Of the 23 most popular women's health apps assessed by Alfawzan et al., 70\,\%
displayed a privacy policy, 87\,\% shared user data with third parties and
57\,\% informed users about data security~\parencite{alfawzan2022privacy}. Shipp
and Blasco analysed the policies of 30 Android menstrual apps and found that
reproductive data was mostly not covered by them, although the apps needed it to
work~\parencite{shipp2020period}. Mehrnezhad and Almeida evaluated the privacy
notices and tracking of 30 fertility apps against the
GDPR~\parencite{mehrnezhad2021caring}. Krumbholz et al.\ followed whether the
policies of more than 30 period trackers changed their language after the
\emph{Dobbs} decision~\parencite{krumbholz2024tracking}, and Hammond and Burdon
examined how the control-based protections of the Australian Privacy Act apply
to the policies of 20 menstrual apps~\parencite{hammond2024intimate}.

\paragraph{Policies against behaviour and labels.} A second line compares what
policies say with what apps do. Huckvale et al.\ found that only 12 of 28 apps
sending data to Google and 6 of 12 sending data to Facebook named the company
in their policies~\parencite{huckvale2019assessment}; earlier, 38 of 49
accredited health apps that transmitted information and had a policy did not
describe the personal information included in the
transmissions~\parencite{huckvale2015unaddressed}. Similar gaps appear in femtech
apps~\parencite{erickson2022expecting} and in mental health apps, where each of 25
apps embedded at least one tracker its policy did not name~\parencite{georgiou2026mind}.
The store labels introduced by Apple and Google repeat the problem at scale:
81\,\% of nearly 5{,}000 Android apps misrepresented collection or sharing in the
Data Safety section~\parencite{arkalakis2024abandon}, 97\,\% of iOS apps labelled
\emph{Data Not Collected} had a policy indicating otherwise~\parencite{ali2024honesty},
and across 6{,}051 Android apps, policy and label disagreed most for sensitive
data and for sharing~\parencite{khatun2026disclosure}. For telehealth apps, the
Data Safety section raised users' confidence in their expectations without
making them more accurate, and users overestimated their legal
protection~\parencite{frik2026bitesize}.

\paragraph{Interfaces and several surfaces.} Fewer studies look at the
interface. Hutton et al.\ assessed self-tracking apps and their policies against
privacy heuristics~\parencite{hutton2018assessing}. Malki et al.\ combined a
thematic analysis of the Data Safety sections and policies of 20 female health
apps with a privacy-focused usability inspection of the apps, and found
inconsistencies between policy content and privacy-related app features, flawed
consent and deletion mechanisms and covert collection of sensitive
data~\parencite{malki2024exploring}. Pybus et al.\ combined an audit of app
manifests, a review of policies and Data Safety sections and walkthroughs of the
interfaces of 14 baby-tracking apps, all of which shared data with third
parties~\parencite{pybus2026extraction}. Bahrini et al.\ studied the privacy
policies, data sharing and consent dark patterns of mHealth apps used in
Germany~\parencite{bahrini2025transparency}. Users' reviews of femtech apps
describe deceptive interface patterns and the harms these
cause~\parencite{alsebayel2025ad}. Other studies compare the policy with the
store description of depression apps~\parencite{singh2023assessment}, audit three
period trackers as a user would, against data-protection and intimate-privacy
criteria~\parencite{white2026experiential}, or score four reproductive health
apps and discuss the risks of IP tracking, advertising partners and possible
subpoenas~\parencite{zadushlivy2025exploration}.

\paragraph{Risk statements in policies.} Only a few studies record whether a
policy speaks of risks at all. LaMonica et al.\ assessed the policies of 34
youth mental-health apps and e-tools: 8 explicitly warned about the privacy risks
of services outside the provider's control, such as third-party advertisements,
and 3 gave some detail~\parencite[Table~4]{lamonica2021privacy}. Malki et al.\
report that 13 of 19 policies stated that law enforcement or regulators might
access user data, but explained little about when this could happen or what
rights users would then have; one policy contradicted the in-app FAQ, which
promised that data would never be shared with the government or law
enforcement~\parencite[\S4.2.5, p.~8]{malki2024exploring}.

\paragraph{Open question.} These studies code data practices: what is
collected, who receives it, whether consent is asked, whether the policy is
available, readable and lawful, and whether it matches the label or the app's
traffic. Where harms appear, they are the researchers' threat model or the
users' concerns, not a unit of the provider's text; the closest exception is a
single checklist item on risk warnings~\parencite{lamonica2021privacy}. Policy
and interface are compared for consistency of practices and features, mostly
for female health apps~\parencite{malki2024exploring}, not for what each surface
says about consequences. RQ1 therefore codes harms, the capabilities that lead to
them and the data flows they need as the units of the provider's communication,
across all kinds of health trackers. RQ4 asks whether the policy and the
interface of the same app differ in these terms, and whether what the policy
discloses can be found where users act in the interface.

\section{Wording of Risk and Protection in Privacy Texts}
\label{sec:rw-framing}

\paragraph{Hedging and downplaying.} A body of work shows that privacy texts can
disclose a practice and still blur its consequences. Pollach described the
communicative strategies with which online privacy policies present data
handling~\parencite{pollach2005typology}. Policies use vague
terms~\parencite{lebanoff2018automatic}, and a corpus study of 350 policies found
that many controllers continue to use strategic indeterminacy under the
GDPR~\parencite{green2025strategic}. In breach notifications, hedge terms such as
\emph{may} or \emph{likely} appeared in 112 of 161 notices (70\,\%), 64 (40\,\%)
claimed to have no evidence of misuse, and only 9 linked the exposed data to
concrete misuse scenarios~\parencite[\S5, p.~7]{zou2019might}. The more severe a
breach, the longer and more complex the notification letter and the fewer
numbers it contains~\parencite{jackson2019breach}. In health, 9 of 19 policies of
female health apps disclaimed responsibility for the practices of third parties
while claiming to vet them, and shifted the review of third-party policies to the
user; all of them stated that the risk of a breach could not be
eliminated~\parencite[\S4.2.7, pp.~8--9]{malki2024exploring}. Promotional materials of
femtech apps frame the apps as empowering their users~\parencite{hendl2022tales},
and Apple's privacy text for its Health app constructs a bounded form of user
control~\parencite{serttas2026bounded}.

\paragraph{Protection claims.} A second body of work questions what protective
measures promise. Martin et al.\ argue that limiting third-party access, using
inferences instead of raw data and keeping data on the device do not prevent
privacy violations when the technique does not address the violating
behaviour~\parencite{martin2025cookies}. An analysis of 257 privacy incidents
reaches a similar conclusion: privacy-enhancing technologies that enable a
functionality still permit the harms inherent in that
functionality~\parencite{veitch2026enhancing}. Descriptions of differential
privacy set users' expectations haphazardly~\parencite{cummings2021description},
and explanations of how the technique works do not set accurate expectations,
whereas explanations of its consequences do better~\parencite{smart2025models}.
Policies can contradict their own commitments elsewhere in the same
text~\parencite{brackin2026privacy}, and the de-identification that health apps
cite as protection is weak against re-identification of wearable
data~\parencite{chikwetu2023deidentification}. Doan et al.\ found that 81\,\% of 62
information flows in genetic-testing policies were vague and that the policies
lacked the collective risks of sharing
data~\parencite{doan2024transparency}.

\paragraph{Calls for risk communication.} Several authors conclude that notices
should communicate harms rather than practices. Ebert et al.\ argue that the
emphasis on disclosing data practices leaves users uncertain about potential
harms and propose \emph{risk transparency}, modelled on risk communication in
drug safety and public health~\parencite{ebert2026procedures}. Moniz and Almeida
gave eight women in Portugal a design-probe kit on privacy and consent in
intimate health technologies; they conclude that a one-size-fits-all notice leads
back to habituation and that privacy mechanisms should inform users of the
specific risks of the processing~\parencite{moniz2025toward}.

\paragraph{Open question.} The wording of risk has been coded for breach
notifications and for privacy policies in general, and the strength of
protection claims has been examined for big-technology PETs, differential
privacy and genetic testing. For health tracking apps, the evidence is
incidental: findings on responsibility shifting and breach disclaimers in one
study of female health apps~\parencite{malki2024exploring} and a discourse
analysis of one provider~\parencite{serttas2026bounded}. No study found here
codes how health app providers word the harms they name, and none relates a
claimed protection to the pathways to harm it would have to interrupt. The calls
for risk transparency~\parencite{ebert2026procedures,moniz2025toward} also lack a
measure of the current state. RQ2 codes whether a named harm is acknowledged,
qualified, minimised, denied or presented as the user's responsibility, with
labels adapted from Zou et al.\ (Section~\ref{sec:axes}). RQ3 records each
protection claim with its scope and strength and checks it against the
pathways of the harm trees.

\section{Language Models for Privacy-Text Analysis}
\label{sec:rw-llm}

Large language models are now used to annotate privacy policies at scale.
Studies evaluate them for annotating data practices~\parencite{rodriguez2024llm},
contextual-integrity parameters~\parencite{chanenson2025automating} and the
transparency requirements of the GDPR at word level~\parencite{cory2026wordlevel},
for assessing policies under recent regulations~\parencite{xie2025evaluating},
for comparing policies with store labels~\parencite{ali2024honesty,khatun2026disclosure},
for extracting the privacy claims of mental health apps~\parencite{georgiou2026mind}
and for detecting contradictions within a policy~\parencite{brackin2026privacy}.
Each reports an evaluation against human annotation. Their tasks concern what is
disclosed, not which harms a text names or how it words them. They inform the
design and validation of the coding in this study (Section~\ref{sec:llm}) rather
than a research question.

\section{Research Gap and Research Questions}
\label{sec:rw-gap}

Taken together, the four strands leave one question open: what health tracking
providers tell their users about the harms their data can lead to. Harms are
documented but not compared with providers' statements; communication is coded
for practices, not harms; the wording of risk and protection is studied outside
health tracking; and language models are validated for practices, not for harms
or stance. The closest studies, Malki et al.\ and LaMonica et
al.~\parencite{malki2024exploring,lamonica2021privacy}, touch on harms in single
findings or a single item. Table~\ref{tab:rw-gap} links each strand to its gap
and to the research question that takes it up.
