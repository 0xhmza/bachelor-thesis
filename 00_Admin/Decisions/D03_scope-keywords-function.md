# D3 — Which apps count as health trackers, and which keywords find them?

- **Status:** proposed (author's choice 2026-10-23: "use the domain of any paper that made a research on health trackers"; awaiting supervisor). Fixed before the 23 October collection. The methodology states this choice.
- **Affects:** Methodology, App Sampling; [sampling protocol](../../04_Study/App-Sample/sampling_protocol.md) v2; [keywords.csv](../../04_Study/App-Sample/keywords.csv); harm trees and per-domain reporting (see "Consequences")
- **Options considered:**
  1. The proposal's 19 author-chosen fitness and fertility keywords (used in the 22 October trial). Rejected: no stated source, which the [app-selection evaluation](../App-Selection-Evaluation_2026-10-23.md) §3 criticised.
  2. A domain defined by store category (Health & Fitness, Medical). Rejected: categories mix trackers with content and shopping apps, and the digest recommends a functional definition (stage A, implication 3).
  3. **The domains of two published app studies on health trackers, with their keyword lists, searched in English and German, plus a functional tracking rule (proposed).**
- **Evidence:**
  - Hutton et al. (2018), *Assessing the Privacy of mHealth Apps for Self-Tracking*: no taxonomy of self-tracking apps existed, so the 20 data types most often reported by 105 London Quantified Self members became search keywords, grouped into categories (Table 2, p. 5). The mHealth categories and keywords are cycling; diet (diet, eating); exercise (exercise, workouts); heart (heartrate, heart rate); mood (mood, happiness); running (walking, running); sleep; step count (steps); weight (weight, body fat). The other categories (spending, time keeping) are not health tracking. Hutton et al. excluded instructional exercise apps "because they capture no data".
  - Hutton et al.'s survey-derived list has no menstruation or fertility category; its most popular app, a period calendar, entered only through "mood" (p. 12). The reproductive domain therefore comes from Malki et al. (2024), *Exploring Privacy Practices of Female mHealth Apps in a Post-Roe World*: terms drawn from prior work and tested in the store search, final set "female health, women's health, reproductive health, fertility, ovulation, menstrual, menstruation, period, contraception, birth control, pregnancy, pregnant" (§3.1, p. 4). Malki et al. excluded apps without a tracking or predictive feature and non-consumer apps.
  - Robillard et al. (2019) required that an app "requires the input of personal information ... over time" (§2.1), the functional tracking rule the digest recommends (stage A, approach 4).
  - Supervisor meeting of 13 October: search with German and English keywords; include mental-health trackers; explain the place of fertility and sleep tracking among health trackers ([organized notes](../Meeting-Notes/2026-10-13_Supervisor-Meeting_Organized.md) §1). Hutton et al.'s mood and sleep categories and Malki et al.'s reproductive terms cover these.
- **Choice (proposed):**
  - **Domain.** Health self-tracking apps in the mHealth categories of Hutton et al. (2018) and the female reproductive-health domain of Malki et al. (2024), including pregnancy apps, as Malki et al. did.
  - **Keywords.** The 15 mHealth keywords of Hutton et al. and the 12 terms of Malki et al., each searched in the original English and in one German translation of the same data type; identical spellings (`workouts`, `menstruation`) and repeated translations are searched once: 49 queries ([keywords.csv](../../04_Study/App-Sample/keywords.csv)). Translations follow the tracked data type rather than the word (`eating` → `Ernährung`).
  - **Function rule (eligibility E1).** The app lets the user record personal health or body data over time, by input or sensor, in at least one of these categories (Robillard et al. 2019; Malki et al. 2024). Instructional or content-only apps, one-off calculators, shops, games without such recording and general-purpose apps are excluded (Hutton et al. 2018).
  - **Boundary cases.** Companion apps of wearables are eligible when the app itself records or displays the tracking data; the device is not analysed (D11, meeting: apps only). Consumer apps for the general public only (Robillard et al.; Malki et al.). Chronic-disease, snoring and other specialised trackers are inside the domain only where they record data in one of the categories; they are not searched for separately, because neither source lists them.
  - Each app's primary category is recorded as a descriptor. There are no quotas (D1).
- **Reason:** A domain and keyword list taken from published health-tracker studies replaces an author-chosen list with a stated, survey-derived source (Hutton et al.) and a store-tested one (Malki et al.), and covers the meeting's request for mental-health (mood) and sleep trackers. Searching each term in English and German follows the meeting. The function rule makes "tracker" a property of the app, not of its store category.
- **Consequences (open, not decided here):** mood trackers form a third group beside fitness and reproductive apps. The methodology's per-domain plans (two harm trees, six walkthroughs and five validation apps per domain, separate reporting) assume two domains and must be revised once the sample's mix is known (supervisor question, [evaluation](../App-Selection-Evaluation_2026-10-23.md) §2).
- **Would change if:** the supervisor wants a narrower domain (fitness and fertility only) or a separate mental-health search; the screening log shows that the function rule cannot be applied consistently from store pages.
