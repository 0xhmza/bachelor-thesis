Chapter 4

Methodology

This chapter states how the study answers its research question: which harms health-tracker providers
communicate, and how they frame protection. Section 4.1 sets out the design and ties each subquestion to its measure. Section 4.2 gives the literature review that supplies the harms and the
methodological precedents, and Section 4.3 builds from it the reference model of what is possible
for a health tracker. Sections 4.4 and 4.5 state which apps are studied and what is collected from
each. Section 4.6 states how two language models code the collected text and how people check
it, and Section 4.7 how the instruments are tested, frozen and validated against human coding.
Section 4.8 closes with ethics, availability and limitations. The chapter states rules; the numbers
that follow from applying them are reported in Chapter 5.

4.1

Study Design

The study asks which harms providers communicate. A harm can only be called missing if the app
could cause it, so the study first fixes what is possible and then reads what is said. It has three parts:
1. A reference model. A generic health tracker holds the functions that the included evidence
documents for health trackers. Harm trees show how these functions can lead to harms
(Sections 4.3.2 and 4.3.3).
2. Applying the model to each app. The privacy policy, the store listing and the walkthrough
show which parts of the model an app has. This decides which harms apply to it (Section 4.6.3).
3. Comparing text and model. The app’s policy and interface text are coded and set against the
applicable pathways. Two language models code all the text; people check every high-stakes
label and code a validation sample, from which the models’ errors are measured (Sections 4.6.1,
4.6.2 and 4.7.3).
Figure 4.1 shows how the three parts form one workflow: the framework is built once for all apps,
and the steps that apply it are repeated for each app. The figure gives the order in time. In particular,
the instruments are tested and frozen (Section 4.7) before the main collection and coding, although
this chapter describes them afterwards.

4.1.1

Research Questions and Approach

The study answers the research question of Chapter 1: What types of downstream privacy harms
do health tracker service providers explicitly acknowledge or downplay when describing their privacy
practices or PETs, and how do they frame the protection these technologies offer? It is divided into four
sub-questions:
RQ1 Coverage. Which data flows, capabilities and harms are mentioned in the captured communication, and which applicable harms in the reference model are not found?
RQ2 Stance. How are named harms acknowledged, qualified, minimised, denied or presented as
the user’s responsibility?
RQ3 Protection. What measures and benefits are claimed, and how do the scope and strength of
these claims relate to the reference model?
RQ4 Surfaces. How do coverage, stance and protection claims differ between privacy policies and
inspected interfaces?
The coding is a directed content analysis: it starts from prior research, which supplies the initial
codes, and adds what the text shows beyond them [40, 51]. Comparing several surfaces of the same
app follows Malki et al., who set store disclosures, policies and app interfaces against each other [57,
p. 4]. Table 4.1 ties each research question to what is measured, the material, the method and its
precedent. The precedents supply the concepts and the general approach; the applicability record,
the coverage levels, the stance labels and the rules for combining model labels are this study’s own
operationalisations, defined in this chapter and tested in the pilot.

4.1.2

Scope and Key Terms

The study analyses what health-tracking apps communicate, not how they behave. Its objects are
the apps of one domain, health trackers (Section 4.4.1), offered on the German market, together
with their privacy policies, store listings and interfaces. Connected devices lie outside the scope: a
wearable’s companion app can enter the sample, but the device itself is not analysed. No user study
is conducted. The interface is inspected by the analyst (Section 4.5.2), and the study tests neither
the apps’ behaviour nor their security.
The chapter uses seven terms throughout; each is developed in the section named.
Health tracker An app that lets users record their own health or body data over time, such as
activity, nutrition, sleep, heart rate, mood or reproductive health; the operational criteria are in
Section 4.4.1.
System component Data the app collects, derives or sends, what the user and others do with the
app, or a requirement it imposes (Section 4.3.3).
Capability What components enable a named actor to do, or what they take away from the user,
an incapability (Section 4.3.1).
Harm An impairment or disruption of an entity’s capacity or ability to function, measured against
the user’s situation without the modelled pathway (Section 4.3.1).
Pathway to harm Components that together enable a capability that results in a harm; a harm tree
combines all pathways to one harm (Section 4.3.3).
Surface A place where a provider communicates about privacy; the coded surfaces are the privacy
policy and the interface text (Section 4.5.1).
Applicability Whether a pathway is possible for a given app, judged from the components the app
has (Section 4.6.3).
No human participants are involved; the safeguards for the walkthroughs are set out in Section 4.8.1.

4.2 Literature Review Procedure

4.2

Literature Review Procedure

The literature review has two tasks in this study. It collects the harms and capabilities from which the
reference harm trees are built, and it supplies the evidence for the methodological decisions of this
chapter. Its procedure follows the staged review of Baumann et al. [10]: fixed Boolean queries built
from term groups, the exact string and hit count reported for every source, removal of duplicates,
screening against explicit criteria and the extraction of fixed fields. The search is reported with the
items of the PRISMA-S extension and the flow of records with the flow diagram of PRISMA 2020 [65,
68]. Because the review maps evidence rather than pooling effects, it is a scoping review in the
sense of Arksey and O’Malley [7].

4.2.1

Review Questions and Search Concepts

Review question. The review asks one question, derived from the RQ “what harms service providers
communicate”: What is known about the privacy harms and capabilities associated with health tracking
apps, about how their privacy communication has been analysed and what it says, about how language
models have been used and validated for coding privacy text, and about how privacy texts frame risk
and protection? It has four sub-questions, each tied to a part of this study.
LQ1 Harms and capabilities. Which privacy harms, risks, threats and inference capabilities concern
the users of health tracking apps and their wearables? It supplies the nodes of the harm trees
and the reference model of RQ1 and RQ3, and starts from general accounts of privacy harm.
LQ2 Communication. How has the privacy communication of health tracking apps been analysed,
and what was found? It informs the sampling, material and coding decisions and tests the gap
for RQ1–RQ4.
LQ3 Language models. How have large language models been used to annotate, classify, extract
from or assess privacy text, and how were they validated? It informs the LLM-assisted coding.
LQ4 Framing. How do privacy texts in any domain word or present risks, harms and protections, for
example through vagueness, hedging, reassurance or misleading claims? It informs the stance
and protection axes (RQ2, RQ3).
Search concepts. The search is built from concept blocks. Each concept of the review question
becomes a term group whose alternative terms are joined with OR, and the groups of a query are
joined with AND, so that a record must contain at least one term of every group. Eight groups cover
the concepts (Table 4.2; the complete term lists are in Appendix F.1). The domain group (D) names
health tracking apps and their wearables, with the domains of the sample in Section 4.4 (activity,
diet, sleep, mood and mental health, cycle, fertility and pregnancy) and the generic terms under
which such apps are studied (mHealth, wearable, self-tracking). The privacy group (P) restricts
the domain to privacy. The harm group (H) names harms and the threats and inferences through
which capabilities are described. The communication group (C) names the surfaces the study codes.
The privacy-text group (T) names policies, labels and notices in any domain; the model group (L)
and the task group (A) select language-model studies that annotate or assess such text; the framing
group (F) names the wording strategies that the stance axis codes.

listed completely from 2014 to the search date (Table F.2): the four main security conferences (CCS,
IEEE S&P, NDSS and USENIX Security), the venues for privacy-enhancing technologies (PoPETs)
and for usable privacy and security (SOUPS), and CHI and CSCW, where user studies of privacy

Platform strings. The strands are the shared search logic; each platform receives them in its own
syntax (Table F.3). The complete strings, fields, date limits and search times are given in Appendix F.3.
PubMed and IEEE Xplore receive each strand as one query. Every term is searched in the title and
the abstract, terms of several words are enclosed in quotation marks as phrases, and a trailing
asterisk truncates; PubMed also accepts truncation inside a quoted phrase. IEEE Xplore requires the
field to be named for each term, so every term occurs twice, once for the document title and once
for the abstract. The arXiv interface returned fewer records for long nested queries than for their
parts, so arXiv receives short anchor queries instead: each pairs one domain word with one privacy
term, or joins the words of one privacy-text term, in the title or abstract. The records of all anchor
queries form a pool. The ACL Anthology is taken as its complete bibliography file with abstracts.
The strands are applied locally to the arXiv pool, to the ACL Anthology file and to the venue lists,
with one matching rule: titles, abstracts and terms are reduced to lower-case letters and digits, a
term matches whole words only, and a trailing asterisk matches any word ending. Date limits are
set at the platform (publication date in PubMed, year in IEEE Xplore, submission date in arXiv) or
applied locally (publication year in the ACL Anthology file and the venue lists). No language or
publication-type filter is set; both are screening rules.
Citation searching. Citation searching adds studies that the term groups miss. It starts from a seed
set of four studies that address the core topic of the review directly: three CHI papers on privacy in
period, fertility and other female health apps [16, 44, 57] and a survey of the utility, privacy and
security of wearable activity trackers [71]. One round of backward searching (the references of the
seed papers) and forward searching (the works that cite them) is run. References and citing works
are taken from the OpenCitations Index and their metadata from Crossref. The records found enter
the same deduplication and screening as the search results.

4.2.3

Screening and Deduplication

Deduplication. The records of all sources are merged automatically by a script of this project. Two
records are taken to be the same work when they share a DOI or an arXiv identifier, or when their
normalised titles (lower-case letters and digits) are identical and their publication years differ by at
most one; three years are allowed when one of the two is an arXiv preprint, which may precede the
published paper by years. The merged record keeps every source that found it, so that the yield of
each source can be reported.
Eligibility. A record is eligible if it meets all four criteria of Table 4.4, which were fixed before
screening.

Extraction and Synthesis

Each study that passes title-and-abstract screening receives one row in an extraction table. The row
records the sub-question the study answers, assigned at screening, and three fields coded from its
title, abstract and venue with fixed keyword lists: the app domain (for example reproductive health,
mental health, or activity and fitness), the material studied (for example privacy policies, privacy
labels, app traffic or interviews with users) and the type of venue. For the studies included after
full-text screening, three fields are coded by hand from the full text: the parts of the research question
the study informs (RQ1 to RQ4, or a methodological precedent), the harms it names and, for LQ1
studies, the capabilities it documents. The capabilities are grouped into a small set of categories,
each recorded with the data it needs and the actor who could use it, for example the re-identification
of individuals from de-identified sensor data by whoever holds the data. This capability table is
the input to the reference harm trees, as the counterpart of the third stage of Baumann et al. The
synthesis is descriptive. The studies are counted by year and venue; an evidence map crosses the
app domains with the materials studied and with the parts of the research question, which shows
where evidence is concentrated and where it is missing; and the findings are summarised in prose
for each sub-question. The capability table and the harms it records feed the analytical framework
(Section 4.3); the methodological precedents are cited where each decision of this chapter is stated.

4.3

Analytical Framework

A harm can only be called missing from an app’s privacy communication if the app could cause it.
The analytical framework therefore fixes, before any app is coded, what is possible. It has three parts,
built in sequence: the harm concepts and categories (Section 4.3.1); a generic health tracker that
holds the functions the included evidence documents for such apps (Section 4.3.2); and reference
harm trees that trace how these functions can lead to harms (Section 4.3.3). The same framework

4.3.1

Harm Concepts and Categories

The framework adopts the vocabulary of Knabenhans et al., who trace harms through three kinds of
elements: system components, capabilities and harms [50, §4.1]. This section defines capabilities,
harms and the categories into which harms are sorted. Components, the leaves of the harm trees,
are defined with the trees (Section 4.3.3).
Capabilities. System components, that is, the data a system leaks, the results of interacting with it
and the requirements it imposes, combine to give someone a capability, or to take one away [50,
§4.1]. In a health tracker, an analytics partner may be able to profile users across apps, and a partner
with access to the phone may be able to infer a pregnancy from the logged cycles. An incapability
counts as well: a user who cannot use the cycle predictions without agreeing to data sharing has
lost a choice. Each capability names the actor who holds it, because the same data leads to different
harms in the hands of a partner, a provider, an attacker or an authority. De and Le Métayer make
the same distinction with their risk sources, the entities whose actions lead to privacy harms [20].
Harms. Capabilities, alone or together, can lead to harms. The study adopts the definition Knabenhans et al. take from the CyberPeace Institute: a harm is an “impairment or disruption of an entity’s
capacity or ability to function”, measured against the entity’s usual context [50, §4.1]. Knabenhans
et al. take current practice in the EU as that context. Here, the usual context is the user’s situation
without the modelled pathway: the same person, using the same kind of app, without that flow of
data or that requirement. Any negative impact counts, on the user, on a group of users or on society,
whether physical, psychological, financial or affecting reputation, dignity, freedom, domestic life or
another fundamental right [50, §4.1].
Categories. The starting categories are Knabenhans et al.’s physical, economic, psychological,
autonomy/deprivation, discrimination, reputational and societal harms [50, App. A], drawn from
Citron and Solove and the CyberPeace Institute. A relationship tag retains Citron and Solove’s
relationship harms [18], which the list omits. New harms found in the pilot will be documented
rather than forced into a category.

4.3.2

Generic Health-Tracker Reference Model

The supervisor asked for a harm tree based on the “best app”. No criterion says which real app is
best, and any real app lacks functions that others have. The study therefore builds the tree on a
generic health tracker: a model that holds the functions that the included evidence documents for
health trackers. It is not a claim to list every function a tracker could have; a function the evidence
does not document is missing from it. A real app is compared with it function by function. If the
model has a function that an app lacks, the pathways through that function do not apply to that
app. The harms are thus judged against one model, and all 50 apps are treated alike. What the
comparison yields is a dated distance between an app’s communication and this reference, not a
judgement that a provider was obliged to describe each pathway.
Precedents. Knabenhans et al. model one functionality that covers many applications, and then
show that harms can only be judged once concrete uses are known [50, §4]. They reuse sub-trees
across harms and across use cases [50, §4.2–4.3]. De and Le Métayer separate a generic analysis,
which yields generic harm trees from the system’s specification, from its later refinement for a

4.3.3

Reference Harm-Tree Construction

The reference harm trees fix which harms are possible for health trackers, and how, before any
text is coded. They follow the method that Knabenhans et al. developed to trace the harms of the
European Digital Identity Framework [50, §4.1]. A harm tree shows how parts of a system combine
into something that someone can do with it, and how that in turn leads to a harm. Harm trees were
introduced by De and Le Métayer, who used them to link the weaknesses of smart-grid systems, the
actors who exploit them and the events they cause to the resulting privacy harms [20]. Here the
system is a health-tracking app together with its provider, its servers and the partners that receive
its data. The trees are built once on the generic model (Section 4.3.2) and then checked against
each app (Section 4.6.3).

System components. Knabenhans et al. define system components as the information that the use
of a system leaks, the results of an entity’s interaction with it, and the requirements it imposes on
the entities [50, §4.1]. For an app, these are three things: the data the app collects, derives or sends,
such as logged cycle dates or a device identifier passed to an analytics partner; what the user and
others do with the app, such as switching on a cloud backup or opening an unlocked phone; and
what the app requires, such as an account, or consent to data sharing before a feature can be used.
In the study, components are taken from prior research and confirmed for each app from its store
listing, its policy or the walkthrough.
Constructing trees. When components enable a capability that results in a harm, they form a
pathway to harm. All pathways to the same harm are combined into one harm tree with three
levels [50, §4.1]: the harm at the top, the capabilities that enable it in the middle, and the components
that enable each capability at the leaves. Operators state how the elements combine. AND means
that every component is needed, as in the left branch of Figure 4.3: the cycles must be logged and
another person must reach the unlocked phone. OR means that any one branch is enough: either
capability leads to the harm. OR+ means that one or several components may be needed together,
without a fixed combination [50, §4.1]. An OR+ cannot be evaluated as it stands, because it does
not say which inputs suffice. Each OR+ of the trees is therefore rewritten before evaluation as an OR
of explicitly sufficient combinations, each an AND of components, as in the set semantics of attack
trees, where a tree denotes the alternative bundles of components that together achieve its goal [59].
Each combination states the actor who gains the capability and the component that gives that actor
access to the data; a combination the sources do not establish is not added, and the capability then
stays uncertain. A prediction computed on the phone, for example, gives no partner a capability
unless a component shows that the partner receives it. In reference model v2, the capability to
sort users into classes (K10) has two such combinations: the provider derives insights and uses
the tracked data for offers or partner products (CO2 and IM7), or an advertising partner receives
identifiers or app events that a persistent identifier links to one user (IM4 and CX3).

Because the operators make every required condition explicit, a tree also shows how a pathway
can be cut: a measure blocks it only if it removes one of its necessary conditions. De and Le Métayer
combine AND and OR nodes to compute how likely each harm is [20, Sec. IV-B]. The trees in this
study are not used for that: they record which harms are possible, not how probable they are.
Harm origins. Each component is marked with its origin, the root cause of the pathways it belongs
to [50, §4.1]. A component belongs to the core functionality when the app cannot do its job without
it, such as the logged cycle dates of a period tracker. It comes from the digital context when it follows
from running on a phone and a network or from the setting of use, such as a device identifier or
another person with access to the phone. It is an implementation choice when the provider decided
it and could decide otherwise, such as a cloud backup, an analytics library, or predictions offered
only in exchange for data sharing. The origin shows who can cut a pathway: a provider can change
its own choices, but cannot remove core components without giving up the function. This is what
the study needs when it compares a provider’s protection claims with the pathways they are meant
to block (Section 4.6.1). The origins are argued from the literature, not proven.
Sources of the trees. The trees are built from prior research on health trackers: a survey of tracker
inferences, threats and vulnerabilities [71], users’ judgements of harm scenarios that the researchers
constructed [29], analyses of policies, data flows and interfaces [44, 57, 75] and post-Roe user
concerns [16], together with the capabilities that the literature review collects (Section 4.2). Sources
found in the logged search are added with a recorded reason. Each pathway names the data, the
actor, the access conditions, the capability and the possible consequence. A prediction made for the
user does not by itself establish access by a third party (Appendix D).

4.4.2

Cross-Store Matching and Popularity Ranking

Same app in both stores. An app listed in both stores is one candidate, so its two listings have to
be found. Names are not enough for this: one developer can offer several similar products, and one
product can carry different titles in the two stores [81, §3.2], which is why Hu et al. matched their
cross-platform apps by hand [41, §3.1]. Following Steinböck et al., two listings are the same app if
they offer the same branded product for the same use [81, §3.2, p. 352]. Pairs are first proposed
from several signals, as in their matcher [81, §3.2.2, p. 353]: in every search frame that the selection
or a sensitivity check uses, a Google Play and an App Store listing are proposed if they share the
developer, the app identifier (the App Store bundle ID equals the Google Play package name), the
title, or at least 20 passages of four words of description text that occur in at most ten listings. A
proposal decides nothing. Each proposed pair is then decided on product-specific evidence, which
is recorded with the decision: an official page that links both listings, an identical app identifier,
the same product name in both titles or in both English store names, shared description text, or
one listing that names the product of the other. A shared developer, web domain or privacy policy
is no such evidence, nor is a generic title such as “Schrittzähler”, and a different target group or
main function makes a different app: Nike Run Club and Nike Training Club are two apps of one
developer. Rating counts and the order of the listings play no part. Both listings of one app belong
to one provider, so the developer rule below treats their two developer accounts as one provider. A
pair that the evidence does not decide stays unresolved and is not merged, as some pairs could not
be classified by Steinböck et al. [81, §5.2, p. 356]; the sample counts as final only if merging every
unresolved pair would change none of the selected listings, in the main selection or in any check.

score, Pi , calculated from the feedback counts of app i in the German storefronts: ratings count in
the App Store and written reviews count on Google Play. Feedback counts are used because they

Preinstalled apps. Health apps that ship with the phone reach users without a download, so their

app, if it has one, is removed from the store rankings, and its vendor counts as a provider already
in the sample, so that the vendor’s other apps are skipped by the developer rule. The vendors and
apps found are reported in Section 5.2.

4.5.2

Cognitive Walkthrough

A privacy policy says what a provider has written down. The interface shows what a user meets, and
whether the user can find it. To inspect it, the study uses a cognitive walkthrough: an analyst steps
through a task and asks at each step whether a user would succeed [88]. The walkthrough supplies
interface evidence alongside the policy and store listing. Its tasks are tied to nodes of the reference
harm trees, its interface text is coded with the same codebook and the same model-assisted stages as
the policy, and what it shows about the app enters the same applicability record. The walkthrough
itself is done by hand. The language model plays no part in it: it does not operate the app, answer
the four questions or judge an outcome.
The method examines how an interface supports learning by exploration. Here it assesses the
steps for finding privacy information and using controls. All variants of the method ask about goals,
actions, feedback and completion, but they are thinly validated, so an adaptation has to be stated
openly [56, pp. 15, 20–22]. The study keeps the four questions and applies them to the privacy
tasks, with bounded search and the outcome categories stated above. It does not add Nielsen’s
heuristics: the focus is access to tree-related information and controls, rather than a general design
audit. Malki et al. combined both methods and used two independent inspectors [57, p. 5]. One
analyst can carry out the method alone [88, p. 109], but results depend on the analyst’s expertise,
and recording is advised [56]. The sessions are therefore recorded and the analyst’s background
reported. A second analyst then rates the recorded sessions of a random subset of apps on their own:
for each action, the answers to Q1–Q4 and each task’s outcome, without seeing the first analyst’s
record. This tests the judgments without repeating the inspection, which would need the same
device, accounts and app versions. It tests the interpretation of the recorded routes, not whether
the first analyst missed a route that was never recorded. The second analyst is the second coder of
Section 4.7.3. The subset is ten apps, drawn with a recorded seed.
Decision of 4 November 2026 (D8, D10): ten of the 50 apps, a fifth. Double-coding 10–25 % of the data units is typical for intercoder
reliability [63, p. 5]; a fifth sits in that range, and at about 45 minutes per recorded session it costs the second coder about eight hours.
The second analyst is the second coder of Section 4.7.3.

At each step of a task, the analyst answers the four questions of Wharton et al. [88, Table 5.1,
p. 106]:
Q1 Will the user try to achieve the right effect?
Q2 Will the user notice that the correct action is available?
Q3 Will the user associate the correct action with the effect they want?
Q4 After the action, will the user see that progress is being made?
For each action, the analyst records yes, no or uncertain for each question, with a reason tied to
the screen and the assumed user’s knowledge. These are judgments about likely difficulties, not
observed user behaviour. A negative answer is retained even if the analyst can continue and complete
the task.

4.6

Coding and Assessment

The trees say what is possible for health trackers in general; the analysis asks what holds for each
app. The captured text is first cut into units and coded on three axes (Section 4.6.1) by two language
models, whose high-stakes labels people check (Section 4.6.2). Three questions then follow in a
fixed order. Step 1 asks which parts of the reference model the app has (Section 4.6.3). Step 2
asks how far the app’s policy and interface text reach up the applicable pathways, and Step 3
whether its protection claims would cut them (Section 4.6.4). The first two steps are answered
before any comparison of protection claims, so that a claim cannot shape what counts as applicable.
Section 4.6.5 sets out how the results are reported for each research question.

Axis A: Coverage. All explicitly mentioned nodes are recorded, including whether each is asserted,
presented as possible or denied. A denial is a mention with negative polarity, not an absence: a
statement that a provider does not share data says something different from silence about sharing,
which is why automated policy analysis models negative statements explicitly [1, §1]. Polarity is
therefore kept as an attribute of the mention and is never folded into whether a node was mentioned.
The highest level reached is kept as a separate summary, so that a harm mention does not erase
accompanying data-flow information. Feature descriptions can mention inference without presenting
it as a risk.
Axis B: Stance. Stance labels are linked to the particular harm and its supporting span, not assigned
to the paragraph as a whole. A statement may acknowledge a harm and qualify its likelihood,
and the coder distinguishes uncertainty about a breach from uncertainty about its consequences.
Minimisation is judged from wording in the unit or adjacent text, never from inferred intent;
qualification alone does not establish it. The label therefore measures an explicit dismissal that the
local text does not support; it does not measure whether the provider understates an actual risk,
which the study cannot know. A “no evidence” statement (“there is no evidence that your data was
affected”) is coded as qualification when it reports the basis of the judgement and as minimisation
when it dismisses the harm itself. RQ2 thus covers stance toward named harms; dismissive language
without a named harm is coded as reassurance, and responsibility placed on the user without a
named harm as generic responsibility, both for the secondary analysis (Section 4.6.5). The five
stance labels are this study’s adaptation of the risk communication that Zou et al. examined [93,
pp. 7, 10]; they are not a validated scale, and their boundaries are refined in the pilot.
Axis C: Protection. The coder records the measure and its claimed benefit, with actor, data and
conditions where specified. Any technical measure claimed to limit exposure or misuse counts,
whether or not it is labelled a privacy-enhancing technology, including ordinary security controls;
non-technical commitments are kept separate. A potential mismatch requires a pathway within the
claim’s scope and an explanation of why the measure misses one of its necessary conditions; partial
protection is not rejected merely because other risks remain. Vague scope is recorded as unclear,
and no deployed system is judged safe or unsafe.

4.6.2

LLM Coding with Two Models and Human Checks

The corpus is too large for a person to read and code in full within this study: the one policy of the
instrument test alone has 178 units (Section 5.4). Language models therefore do the coding. Polisis,
a hierarchy of deep-learning classifiers, and recent LLM approaches are precedents for data-practice
analysis [37, 70]. Large language models have annotated privacy policies with results close to
those of experts [17], and have extracted and assessed legally required policy content with high
performance against human-labelled benchmarks [91, §§4.1–4.3]. They have not been shown to
code named harms or stance, and a field report on qualitative content analysis found only rough
approximations with gross errors [60]. Performance depends on the task, so it has to be validated
against human labels [66], and model labels used directly as data bias the estimates built on them
and make their intervals invalid, even when the model is accurate on most units [25, §2]. The
design therefore has three parts: two models code all the text; people check the labels on which
the findings depend most; and a human-coded validation sample measures the models’ errors and
corrects the reported figures (Section 4.7.3).
Two models. Two models from different model families code every unit independently, with the
same codebook, stages and prompts. Both are selected in the pilot and then frozen, with versions

32

4.6 Coding and Assessment

and parameters reported; an open-weight model is preferred for one of them if it performs close to
a hosted one [84]. The second family is there because the errors of language models are correlated;
in the datasets Kim et al. studied, the correlation was higher between models of the same provider
or architecture [48, §§3–4]. A model of another family can therefore be expected to catch some
of what the first misses, and where the two disagree the text is likely to be ambiguous or one of
them wrong. How much the second model actually adds is measured on this corpus (Section 4.7.3),
not assumed. For the same reason, agreement between the two models is reported as a robustness
check and never as reliability: two models can agree on the same error.
Stages and evidence. Each model codes in three stages. Stage 1 codes tree nodes, named harms
and generic risks; Stage 2 codes the stance toward each named harm; Stage 3 codes the details of
each protection claim. The stages adapt the hierarchical decomposition of Polisis, which classifies
broad categories before their attributes [37, §4.3]; the three stages themselves are this study’s
design. Every label quotes its span, as a decision and the supporting text, as in the exact-text
extraction of Xie et al. [91, App. B]. A script checks that the span occurs in the unit; a label without
a valid span is discarded from the data but counted as a failure of the model, so that it still lowers
measured performance. An uncertain answer is kept as uncertain and never becomes a negative
code [84]. The prompts give the context, then the codebook definitions, then the output format, as
Rodriguez et al. tested [70, §4.2, pp. 3886–3888]; the two constructed examples, the absence of a

in the pilot measure each model’s stability, following the repeated runs of Rodriguez et al. [70, §4.3,
pp. 3888–3889]; the number of repetitions is fixed in the pilot. A paraphrased prompt run on the
validation sample measures how much the wording changes the labels [10]. The complete rules,
each with its precedent, are in Table B.1 (Appendix B).
Combining the two models. The labels of the two models are combined by the rules of Table 4.12.
They distinguish routine labels from high-stakes labels. Routine labels record what the text says

4.6.3

Applicability Assessment (Step 1)

Step 1 asks which parts of the reference model an app has. An applicability record gives every
component one of three states: present, absent or uncertain, with its evidence (policy, listing or
walkthrough, with location) and a reason. Text can show that a feature exists; it cannot show that
a feature works, so what only the app can show stays uncertain unless the walkthrough supplies
sufficient evidence about an observable condition. A non-finding alone does not establish absence
or incapability. Events outside the app are not observed. An attacker gaining access is assumed
possible for stored data. An authority’s demand and an insurer’s or employer’s use stay uncertain
unless the evidence shows them, because the study does not assume the legal setting of another
country for German users. Each state records whether it rests on the app’s evidence or on one of
these assumptions, so that the reader can see which applicable pathways the assumptions create. An
incapability is set from the absence of any usable route, not from a missing button: the components
that no usable way exists to delete the data (IM12) or to export it (IM14) are present only if neither
the app nor another route, such as a request to the provider, offers it, and deletion and export are
judged separately. A missing in-app control is reported as a walkthrough finding.

A script then evaluates the trees. AND is absent if any part is absent, and present only if all parts
are present. OR is present if any part is present, and absent only if all parts are absent. Everything else
stays uncertain. An OR+ is evaluated only in its rewritten form, as an OR of sufficient combinations
(Section 4.3.3); asking whether a pathway is possible at all does not make a single input sufficient.
A harm category applies when at least one of its pathways does. Uncertain cases are counted and
reported, not silently dropped, so that sparsely documented providers do not vanish from the gap
analysis. The trees are reference models of possible harms, not complete descriptions of the apps’
actual systems.

Reporting by research question. Results are descriptive. For RQ1, each harm’s denominator

not offer is recorded as such; only sources that exist but cannot be retrieved reduce the denominator.

the app: each RQ1 proportion is computed per app and then averaged with equal weight for every
app, so that a provider who documents many pathways does not dominate the result; proportions
pooled over all pathways are reported as a secondary view.
Decision of 4 November 2026 (D18): adopted. RQ1 asks how apps communicate, so the app is the unit of the claim. Pooling over
pathways weights an app by its number of applicable pathways, which grows with what its provider discloses, so the most transparent

4.7

Instrument Validation

The instruments (the reference model and trees, the codebook, the walkthrough protocol and the
prompts) are developed in two rounds before the main study and then checked independently of

4.7.2

The pilot takes place after sampling and after the reference harm trees have been built. Five apps are
drawn at random from the full health-tracker sample with a recorded seed, without category quotas.
The pilot tests the boundaries of collection and coding and measures capture and transcript-checking

left open in this chapter: for the walkthroughs, the platform rule, device and operating system,
starting states, route-preparation and stopping rules, time limits, synthetic inputs, the consent and
permission profile, and the lay scenario and completion criteria of each task and subgoal; for the
coding, the stance definitions and the handling of new harms; and the two language models and

the validation sample and the vocabulary of stratum V3 before the sample is drawn. The instruments
are then frozen as one versioned bundle: codebook, prompts and output schemas, the human

4.7.3

Validation Sample, Agreement, and Error Estimates

The validation measures how often the frozen models are wrong, so that the reported figures can be
corrected for it. Its target is the independent corpus: all coding units of the apps outside the pilot
and the instrument test. Its unit is the coding unit, not the app: the errors that matter are rare and
concentrated in few units, and ten whole apps would hold too few of them to estimate anything.
The instrument test illustrates this: its policy of 178 units names no harm at all (Section 5.4).
Validation sample. After both models have coded the independent corpus, every unit is assigned
to exactly one stratum, the first of the following that it meets:
V1 the two models disagree on any code;

V2 a model returned an uncertain label;
V3 no high-stakes label, but harm-related vocabulary in either language, where missed mentions
are most likely;
V4 an interface unit not assigned above, so that performance is measured on interface text and

V5 every remaining unit.
Within each stratum, units are drawn by simple random sampling without replacement with a
recorded seed; a stratum smaller than its allocation is taken whole. The strata are disjoint, so each
unit’s probability of selection is exactly the share of its stratum that is sampled [55, chs. 3, 6], and
every unit of the independent corpus has a positive probability, as the correction requires [25, §2].
Stratum V3 replaces a separate missed-mention audit, and stratum V5 exposes the blind spots of the
vocabulary. Oversampling the units on which the models disagree or are unsure follows the principle
of active and confidence-driven inference, which spends human labels where the model is least
reliable and keeps estimates valid through the selection weights [30, 94]; stratification also narrows
the intervals when model accuracy differs across strata [28]. The numbers of units, independent
providers and unique documents in each stratum are reported. The sample holds 500 units. They are
allocated in proportion to each stratum’s size times the spread of the model errors in it, as estimated
in the pilot (Neyman allocation), [55, ch. 3], with at least 150 units in stratum V5 and at least 30 in
every other stratum, or the whole stratum if it is smaller. If the pilot’s time per unit means that 500
units would take a coder more than 12 hours, the total is reduced to what fits, under the same rule.
Decision of 4 November 2026 (D9). Size: at about a minute per unit, 500 units take each coder some 8 to 9 hours, about 7 % of
a corpus of roughly 45 policies of 150 units. Allocation: Neyman allocation minimises the variance for a given size; with plausible
spreads it puts about half the sample into V5, because V5 is large even though its errors are rare. The floor of 150 in V5 bounds
what a clean-looking V5 can hide: with no error among 150 units, the Jeffreys upper bound on its error share is 1.7 %. Checked on
constructed corpora in scripts/validation-sample/v001.

Two human coders. The supervisor asked for two coders. The author and a second coder code every
unit of the validation sample independently, under the frozen codebook and without model output.
Both work on the same units, which the script fixes before coding, because unclear units are a main
source of false disagreement [15]; double-coding a subset rather than all material is common when
resources are limited [63]. Agreement is computed before any discussion, as Krippendorff’s α for
each code on binary unit indicators (the code applies to the unit or not), unweighted over the sample;
because the sample is enriched with difficult units, the values describe this sample and not the
corpus. A code without variation in the sample is reported as undefined, not as reliable. Prevalence
and raw counts are reported beside α, since rare codes can depress chance-corrected agreement
despite high raw agreement, as shown for κ [27, pp. 543, 545–546]. Following Krippendorff’s
guidelines, a code is taken as reliable with α ≥ 0.800 and as tentative with 0.667 ≤ α < 0.800 [51,
pp. 356–357]; these are cautious conventions, not proof that labels are true [61]. A code below
0.667 is reported only qualitatively; if it is redefined or merged instead, that is a new instrument
version, the affected material is recoded, and its evaluation on this sample is labelled exploratory.
Disagreements are then settled, and the settled labels are the reference labels of the sample. To
estimate intra-coder consistency, the author recodes a random tenth of the sample after at least two
weeks. The second coder also checks the reference trees before the freeze, a random sample of
applicability, minimisation and mismatch decisions that includes decisions judged negative, and the
recorded walkthroughs of ten apps. If no second coder is available, the double coding is reduced
to a smaller probability sample drawn across all strata, and the claims are narrowed accordingly;
coding only the hard strata would not measure reliability across the corpus. The second coder is a
student of a computing programme who has no other part in the thesis. Before coding the sample,

The last section gathers what applies to the design as a whole: its ethical safeguards and feasibility
limits, where its data and instruments are kept, and the limits on what it can show.

4.8.1

Ethical Considerations and Feasibility

No human participants are involved: the material consists of public documents and the researcher’s
own use of publicly available apps. Store terms are checked before any automated retrieval, and
any review requirements are confirmed before collection begins. Walkthroughs use throwaway
study accounts and synthetic data only; no real name, address, health data or payment details are
entered. Paid features, identity verification and paired devices are therefore not obtained for the
study. Where they block a task or the whole inspection, the barrier is recorded, and the app stays in
the document sample (Section 4.5.2).
The pilot’s estimates fix a minimum feasible study before collection: the policies of all apps,
coded by both models; the human check of every high-stakes label; the validation sample; and the
walkthroughs. If time runs short, the second rating of recorded walkthroughs is reduced first and
the interface stratum of the validation sample second; the policy coding, the high-stakes check and
stratum V5 are never reduced, and no stratum is reduced to zero.

4.8.3

Methodological Limitations

Sampling. The sample favours free apps with high German-market popularity scores found by the
chosen keywords and does not represent all health-tracking apps or their users in Germany; one
app per provider also leaves out further apps of large providers. Feedback counts accumulate over
different app lifetimes and depend on prompts to rate or review. The common scale assumes a
typical platform distribution and approximately stable platform-specific feedback rates across apps.
StatCounter’s page-view shares are an approximation of the user distribution, and a listing not found
in one store contributes no count from that store. These limits prevent interpreting the score as a
count of users or communication encounters.
Captured material and paths. The corpus is a dated snapshot, and the walkthroughs cover selected
paths; a bounded non-finding is not evidence that a feature is absent.
Framework. The reference trees depend on the chosen literature. The generic model errs towards
listing more than an app has, which the applicability record corrects; it cannot list what the literature
has not documented, and argued links are weaker than documented ones. A provider who documents
more of its data flows makes more pathways applicable, so applicability partly reflects disclosure;
the separate reporting of evidence-based and assumption-based pathways shows how much.
Coding. Two language models code the corpus, and people read only the high-stakes labels and
the validation sample. Unit-level figures are therefore estimates, corrected for the models’ measured
errors; their intervals are wide for rare codes, and recall for the rarest codes may not be estimable
at all. App- and pathway-level figures are detection summaries and are not corrected; no whole-app
audit is run, so app-level misses are not estimated. A human check of a high-stakes label is the
judgement of one person. The two models’ errors are correlated, so their agreement is no evidence
