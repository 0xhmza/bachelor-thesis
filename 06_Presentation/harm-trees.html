Foundations · 1 · the ancestor

Attack trees: a goal, broken into ways to reach it

Schneier (1999) puts the attacker’s goal at the root. Each child is one way to reach its parent.

OR any one child is enough.

AND every child is needed.

Leaves can carry values (possible, cost) that pass up to the root. A defence helps when it cuts every branch.

Schneier, “Attack Trees”, Dr. Dobb’s Journal 24(12), 1999 · not yet in the project corpus: check before citing · tree constructed for this deck

Attack trees are the notation harm trees borrow: a goal at the root, refined into alternatives (OR) and steps that are all needed (AND); leaf values propagate upward and show which defence removes which attack. The example goal is from the health-tracker domain and constructed. Schneier’s article is not in the corpus; check it before citing.

Foundations · 3 · what counts as a harm

A harm is measured against how life would otherwise go

CyberPeace Institute 2024, via Knabenhans et al.

An “impairment or disruption of an entity’s capacity or ability to function”, compared with its usual context.

Individuals, groups or society; well-being, reputation, dignity, freedom or any other fundamental right.

Baseline in the paper

Current practice in the EU, where the digital identity is not yet widely used.

Baseline in the thesis

The user’s situation without the modelled pathway: the same person, without that flow of data.

Knabenhans et al. 2026, §4.1 (p. 6) and §4.2, citing CyberPeace Institute 2024 [14] and Citron and Solove 2022 [11] · thesis, “Harm Categories”

Knabenhans et al. (§4.1, p. 6) adopt the CyberPeace Institute’s definition: an impairment or disruption of an entity’s capacity or ability to function and exist as it otherwise would in its usual context. Any negative impact counts, on individuals, groups or society. Their baseline is current EU practice (§4.2). The thesis sets the baseline to the user’s situation without the modelled pathway.

Knabenhans et al. 2026 · the setting

Is privacy technology enough to make the EU digital identity safe?

The system

Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 sets up a digital identity wallet: issuers give credentials, users show attributes such as “over 18” to relying parties. The promised safeguard is privacy by design, with PETs such as anonymous credentials.

What the paper does

§3 formal model of the best privacy-by-design version
§4 harm trees: age verification, driving licence
§5 three capabilities every such system has

⌕
Finding: PETs reduce what flows, but many harms stay, because they come from the functionality or its digital context.

Knabenhans, Veitch, Raynal, Stadler, Chatel, Lueks, Troncoso, “On the (Privacy) Harms of the European Digital Identity Framework”, Cryptology ePrint 2026/867, abstract and §1

The EUDIF is introduced by Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 amending eIDAS 910/2014. Contributions (§1): a formal model of the core functionality from regulations and specifications (§3); harm trees for age verification and mobile driving licences (§4); three fundamental capabilities (§5). The abstract concludes that PETs reduce information flows but fall short in mitigating the harms. Section numbers follow the PDF in the corpus; a revision of 16 September exists.

Knabenhans et al. §4.1 · the tree

Three levels: harm, capability, component

Level 1

The harm, at the top.

Level 2

Capabilities that enable it, including incapabilities.

Level 3

System components: leakage, interactions, requirements.

OR+

One or several components, possibly together.

Knabenhans et al. 2026, §4.1 “Tree elements”, “Constructing trees” and Fig. 4 (p. 6) · generic tree drawn for this deck

System components are what the system leaks, what entities do when interacting with it, and what it requires. Components combine into a capability or the lack of one (tracking users; a provider unable to serve a region; a user unable to access a service). Capabilities lead to harms. A pathway is found when components enable capabilities that result in a harm; pathways combine into a tree: harm at the top, capabilities second, components at the leaves. AND and OR show how components combine; OR+ is written when more than one component might be needed.

Knabenhans et al. §4.1 · harm origins

Each leaf says where a pathway comes from, and who could cut it

Core functionality · black

What the minimal function leaks or requires, such as the relying party learning a function of the age. Not removable without the function.

Digital context · blue

What comes from using the function online: cookies, fingerprinting, advertising identifiers.

Implementation · red

A design decision, such as a persistent identifier in a naive implementation. Here PETs and design changes can act.

!
Mobile driving licence: no pathway depends on a red component, so privacy technology cannot cut any of them (§4.3).

Knabenhans et al. 2026, §4.1 “Harm origins”, §4.2 and §4.3; dark-red deployment requirements in §4.4

Black components are inherent to the core functionality and cannot be eliminated without giving up the function. Blue components come from the digital context, e.g. cookies, fingerprinting, advertising IDs (§4.2). Red components are implementation choices, e.g. persistent identifiers in a naive implementation; §4.4 adds deployment requirements in dark red. For the mobile driving licence, no pathway depends on a design decision, so privacy technologies cannot mitigate them (§4.3). The thesis uses the same three origins: core, context, implementation.

Knabenhans et al. §5 · fundamental capabilities

Three capabilities come with the function itself

§5.1

Distinguishing

Telling users apart by what they show, e.g. their nationality.

Tracker: separating users who log a pregnancy from those who do not.

§5.2

Trackability

Linking the same user across different events.

Tracker: one device identifier sent with every analytics event.

§5.3

Inference

Learning attributes that were never shown.

Tracker: a late period suggesting a pregnancy.

⚿
Defined as security games. The paper argues they follow from the functionality, so a PET can only limit them, e.g. by restricting what may be asked.

Knabenhans et al. 2026, §5.1–5.3 · blue tracker examples are this deck’s reading, not the paper’s

§5 analyses three harm-enabling capabilities with security games: distinguishing (keeping the case where users have different attribute values, e.g. nationality), trackability (linking a user between events) and inference (inferring attributes; argued to be inherent, restrictable only by limiting which functions and attributes relying parties may ask for). The blue tracker examples correspond to the thesis codes K-INF and K-TRK.
